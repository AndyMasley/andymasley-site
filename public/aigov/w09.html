Phase II · authored

Optimization and trainability

Read

  • Dive into Deep Learning, the trainability sections (Zhang, Lipton, Li & Smola, d2l.ai) — Ch. 12: 12.1, 12.3–12.6, 12.10 Adam, 12.11 Learning Rate Scheduling; plus 5.4 Numerical Stability and Initialization, 8.5 Batch Normalization, 8.6 ResNet. Write the SGD, momentum, and Adam updates side by side from memory, then check against 12.4, 12.6, and 12.10.
  • Deep Residual Learning for Image Recognition (He, Zhang, Ren & Sun, 2015, arXiv:1512.03385) — §1 with Figure 1 (the degradation problem), §3.1–3.3, §4.1 with Figure 4 and Table 1. Shut the paper and trace one residual block forward and backward with explicit shapes, through the shortcut too, then check against §3.2.
  • Understanding Deep Learning (Prince, udlbook.github.io) — optional. 6.1–6.5 fitting models, 7.4–7.5 backpropagation and initialization, 11.3–11.6 residual networks and batch normalization. Read for failure mechanisms rather than recipes.

Do

Take one small net (an MLP or small CNN on Fashion-MNIST is plenty) and train it four ways: plain SGD, SGD with momentum, Adam, and Adam with a decay schedule. Same data, same batch size, same epoch budget, only the optimizer changes. Plot the four loss curves on one set of axes. Then set the learning rate 10× too high and write down the curve you expect before you run it.

Then write a controlled ablation report, one page plus curves. Pick one intervention (batch normalization, a residual connection, or a learning-rate schedule) and train the same net with and without it, every other hyperparameter frozen, at least three seeds per arm. Report per-arm mean and spread of the final metric, overlay the curves, and give a verdict.

Done when the report states whether the intervention's effect exceeds seed-to-seed noise, with the numbers shown, so a reader can check the verdict against your table without rerunning anything. Keep the code; W10 points the same harness at statistics.

Skip if you can already write the Adam update from memory, explain ResNet Figure 1 without notes, and state what a residual connection changes about the gradient path.

Take away

  • A deep network is trainable when gradients arrive at every layer with usable scale. Momentum and Adam reshape the step, schedules resize it over time, initialization sets the scales at step zero, normalization holds them during training, and residual connections give the gradient a path it cannot lose.
  • None of that changes what the network could express, only whether optimization can find it.
  • Residual connections don't help by making the model bigger. An identity shortcut adds no parameters (ResNet §3.2 says so explicitly), and Figure 1's 56-layer plain net posts higher training error than the 20-layer one. Capacity was never the bottleneck.
  • With y = F(x) + x, every block gets a short route to the loss, and "learn a change to your input" (including no change) becomes the easy default.
  • Backpropagation multiplies roughly one Jacobian per layer, so a per-layer scale factor s compounds as s⁵⁰ over 50 layers: slightly below 1 vanishes, slightly above 1 explodes. Initialization sets s near 1 at step zero; batch normalization re-standardizes activations throughout training so drift can't compound.
  • Momentum keeps an exponential moving average of past gradients and cancels SGD's ravine oscillation. Adam keeps two averages, of the gradient and of its square, giving each parameter its own step scale.
  • EU AI Act Article 53(1)(a) and Annex XI require general-purpose model providers to document "training methodologies and techniques, the key design choices including the rationale and assumptions made".
  • "We adopted X for improved training stability" asserts an ablation result. Annex XI's rationale-and-assumptions line is the hook that lets a reviewer ask how many seeds and what the spread was.
Check yourself
  1. A fixed learning rate can be simultaneously too small and too large over one training run. How, and what does a schedule change?
  2. In a 50-layer plain net, why does the initialization scale decide whether gradients vanish or explode, and what does batch normalization change about that picture?
  3. ResNet Figure 1 shows a 56-layer plain net with higher training error than a 20-layer one. Why does that rule out overfitting, and how do residual connections fix the real problem?
  4. Your single-seed ablation shows batch normalization adding 1.4 points of accuracy. What must the report show before "batchnorm helps this net" is a supported claim?

Answers. (1) Early in training, large steps make fast progress, so a small fixed rate wastes the budget; late in training, minibatch noise means large steps bounce around the minimum instead of settling into it. A decaying schedule serves both regimes in sequence, which no constant rate can do. (2) Backpropagation multiplies roughly 50 layer-Jacobians, so a per-layer scale factor s compounds as s⁵⁰, vanishing below 1 and exploding above it; initialization sets s, and variance-preserving initialization puts it near 1 at step zero. Batch normalization re-standardizes activations at every layer throughout training, so scale drift can't compound and training is far less sensitive to the initial choice. (3) Overfitting predicts low training error with high test error; here training error itself is worse, so the deeper net can't fit the data it has, with capacity to spare. With y = F(x) + x the shortcut passes gradients straight through (the identity term's derivative is the identity), so every block has a short path to the loss, and doing nothing means driving F toward zero rather than learning an identity map through stacked nonlinear layers. (4) Both arms rerun over multiple seeds, per-arm mean and spread reported, and the between-arm gap shown against the seed-to-seed spread. If 1.4 points sits inside seed noise, the claim isn't supported.
