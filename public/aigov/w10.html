Phase II · authored

Experimental craft

Read

  • Evan Miller, "Adding Error Bars to Evals" (Anthropic, 2024, arXiv:2411.00640) — §2 analysis framework, §3 variance reduction, §4 comparing models (unpaired vs. paired), §5 power analysis. Write the standard error of an eval score and of a paired difference from memory, then check §2.1 and §4.2.
  • D2L §22.10, Statistics (d2l.ai) — 22.10.1 evaluating estimators, 22.10.2 hypothesis testing, 22.10.3 confidence intervals, with runnable code for all three. Rerun the CI code at a tenth of the sample size and watch what moves and what doesn't.
  • Full Stack Deep Learning 2022, Lectures 2–3 (fullstackdeeplearning.com/course/2022) — optional. Development Infrastructure & Tooling, then Troubleshooting & Testing. The tooling walkthroughs have aged; watch for the habits, not the tools.

Do

Point your W9 ablation harness at statistics. Run one configuration across several seeds and compute the mean, the CLT standard error, and a 95% CI. Then implement Miller's paired comparison between two configurations: a CI on the per-seed differences, not two CIs eyeballed for overlap. Finish with a power calculation: how many seeds to detect a 1-point effect at 80% power?

Then re-run the W9 ablation as a preregistered mini-study. Write down before anything runs: the hypothesis, with a direction and the minimum effect size you'd care about; the number of seeds, chosen by a power argument you show; and the replication criterion. Run it, report the effect with its CI, and close with a one-paragraph limitations section.

Done when a reader who is not you can rerun the study from the report alone (code reference, seeds, environment, exact metric) and decide from the replication criterion whether they replicated, without asking you. Commit the hypothesis-and-criterion section before the first run and cite that commit in the report.

Skip if you can already produce the correct CI given a score, an n, and a clustering structure, and say when a paired design beats an unpaired one.

Take away

  • An eval score is the sample mean of a random variable: questions drawn from an unseen super-population, answers drawn from the model's sampling distribution. That framing buys CLT standard errors, paired comparisons, and power analysis.
  • A two-point gap can be noise or decisive, and the number alone can't tell you which. Unpaired, two models' error bars on a 500-question eval usually swallow two points whole. Paired on identical questions, Var(A−B) = Var(A) + Var(B) − 2Cov(A,B), and the shared variance cancels.
  • Clustered items (five questions per document, shared templates) make the effective sample size far smaller than the nominal one, so the naive standard error is too small and you end up overconfident. Miller's examples show inflation of more than 3×.
  • Lowering the sampling temperature shrinks variance by measuring a different model (§3.3). Precision bought that way isn't precision about the system you deploy.
  • NIST's Center for AI Standards and Innovation has pre-deployment testing agreements with five frontier labs and more than 40 completed assessments, and in March 2026 signed an MOU with GSA to bring evaluation science into federal AI procurement.
  • The UK AI Security Institute tests leading systems before and after public release, on API access to particular model checkpoints that it notes may carry different safeguards from the public version (Frontier AI Trends Report).
  • When such a score gates a deployment or a purchase, write four things into the funding terms: a standard error on every headline number, paired designs for any model-vs-model claim, seeds and sampling parameters disclosed, and a power analysis showing the eval could have detected the effect.
Check yourself
  1. A model scores 78% on 500 independent questions. Compute the 95% confidence interval. Now suppose those 500 are 100 documents × 5 questions each: what breaks, and in which direction does it bias you?
  2. Why can a paired comparison detect a difference that the two models' separate CIs can't? Point at the formula.
  3. W9 gave you a seed-to-seed SD of about 0.8 points, and you care about 1-point effects. Roughly how many seeds per configuration for 80% power at α = 0.05?
  4. A colleague's ablation changed the optimizer, the schedule, and the data order at once, ran one seed, and credits the optimizer for a 3-point gain. Name both distinct failures.

Answers. (1) SE = √(0.78 × 0.22 / 500) ≈ 1.85 points, so 78% ± 3.6, on the assumption that questions are independent draws from the super-population. With 100 documents × 5 questions, the within-document questions are correlated, the effective sample size is well under 500, and the naive SE comes out too small, leaving you overconfident. Clustered standard errors (Miller §2.2, Appendix A) are the fix, and his examples show inflation of more than 3×. (2) Var(A−B) = Var(A) + Var(B) − 2Cov(A,B). On identical questions the outcomes are positively correlated, so the covariance term shrinks the variance of the difference, sometimes far below what the separate error bars suggest; overlapping CIs therefore do not imply no difference. (3) Two-sample sketch: n ≈ 2(z_{α/2} + z_β)² σ²/δ² = 2 × (1.96 + 0.84)² × 0.64 / 1 ≈ 10 seeds per arm. A paired design needs fewer, which is the practical argument for pairing. (4) Confounding, since three factors changed and the gain can't be attributed to one; and no uncertainty, since one seed leaves 3 points possibly inside seed-to-seed noise. No CI, no claim.
