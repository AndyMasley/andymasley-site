R12: Implementation, budgeting, procurement, enforcement, and crisis

Read

  • OMB Memorandum M-25-22, Driving Efficient Acquisition of Artificial Intelligence in Government (PDF) — acquisition planning, data and IP, testing, risk allocation, portability. Read it beside M-24-18 and list what was dropped.
  • Federal Acquisition Regulation (acquisition.gov) — market research, acquisition planning, competition, contract types, data rights, quality assurance, termination, remedies. Copy the clause numbers you would insert.
  • Standards for Internal Control in the Federal Government (GAO, GAO-14-704G) — the five components and seventeen principles. Use them as the checklist your operating plan has to satisfy.

Do

The case. Six hours ago a frontier developer told your agency that a full set of model weights left its network. Attribution is uncertain, the exfiltration window is not yet bounded, and nothing is public. You brief the principal in one hour: what is known, what is inferred, what decision is needed today, and under which authority. Then write the list of things you needed on hand before that call and did not have. RAND's RRA2849-1 supplies the attacker tiers; SB 53's 15-day Cal OES clock and AI Act Article 55 supply the reporting duties.

Convert your capstone recommendation into an operating plan a real institution could execute: a budget with an account and fiscal-year structure; staffing with grades and realistic hiring timelines; the procurement clauses that produce the data and inspection access your policy assumes; an inspection plan; an enforcement ladder from information demand through penalty to referral; a dashboard with named owners; and a revision schedule with triggers.

Then run the crisis exercise. The core assumption fails at 2 a.m. on a holiday weekend: who decides what, under which authority, on what information, and what gets said publicly within six hours? Write the playbook from whatever the exercise broke.

Done when owners, resources, timelines, authorities, data, controls, consequences, and review triggers are explicit enough for a real institution to execute, and the crisis playbook assigns decision rights instead of directing people to coordinate.

The residency ends here. Snapshot the matrix cells this unit moved (implementation feasibility as a screen on every intervention family), then carry the package into C1.

Take away

  • Authority is not capacity. An authorization creates a program, an appropriation funds it, and the Antideficiency Act makes the gap legally sharp.
  • One-year versus no-year money, FTE ceilings, hiring lead times, and the bar on new starts under a continuing resolution decide whether your policy exists in year one.
  • Procurement is a governance instrument that reaches things rulemaking cannot. A contract clause buys inspection rights, data and model access, portability, and termination remedies, and it binds only the parties who sign.
  • M-25-22 pushes performance-based techniques (statements of objectives, performance work statements, quality assurance surveillance plans), lock-in protections through knowledge transfer and portability, pricing transparency, and standardized data and IP terms.
  • A memo is not enforcement. M-25-22 reaches solicitations issued 180 days after issuance, excludes national security systems, and enforces itself through agency policy updates on a 270-day clock. No penalties attach, the next director can rescind it as it rescinded M-24-18, and durability lives in the statutes and FAR clauses written underneath.
  • The Green Book is the standard your program gets audited against. GAO and the inspector general ask for evidence of controls rather than intentions, so the monitoring and deficiency-reporting path has to exist before launch.
  • Every rung of an enforcement ladder needs its own legal hook: information demand, inspection, warning, civil penalty with a statutory maximum, license or registration consequence, referral to DOJ. Skipping the hook is how enforcement plans die in litigation.
  • Policy relevance: incident reporting is where implementation becomes visible. California's SB 53 requires critical safety incident reports to Cal OES within 15 days; the EU AI Act requires them under Article 73 for high-risk systems and under Article 55 for systemic-risk models, to the AI Office. Both need somebody staffed to read what arrives.
Check yourself
  1. Your policy requires inspection of a vendor's training data. Where does that authority come from if the vendor sells to the government, and where if it does not?
  2. A program is authorized in June and the fiscal year opens under a continuing resolution. What can start and what cannot?
  3. Which contracts does M-25-22 reach, and what happens to it when the OMB director changes?
  4. Name the Green Book components and say which one a dashboard satisfies.

Answers. (1) From the contract: a clause negotiated before award, enforceable against that vendor with termination and remedies attached, which is why procurement is the fastest route to access. Otherwise you need a statutory grant of inspection, subpoena, or civil investigative demand authority, exercised through a rule that survives APA review. (2) A continuing resolution generally continues prior-year activities at prior rates and bars new starts and most reprogramming, so planning, drafting, and hiring against existing authority can proceed while obligating funds for a new program cannot, on pain of an Antideficiency Act violation. (3) Solicitations issued on or after 180 days from 3 April 2025 and options or extensions exercised after that date, excluding AI acquired as a component of a national security system. An OMB memo is rescindable at will, so anything you need to survive belongs in a FAR clause or a statute. (4) Control environment, risk assessment, control activities, information and communication, monitoring. A dashboard is information and communication plus monitoring; it is not a control activity, and treating it as one is a common finding.
