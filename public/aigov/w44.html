Phase IX · authored

Model weights and infrastructure security

Read

  • Securing AI Model Weights (Nevo et al., RAND, May 2024, landing page and direct PDF) — the ~36-page main body: Summary, Chs. 4–7, Figure 4.1, Table 5.1. Rebuild both ladders (OC1–OC5, SL1–SL5) from memory.
  • Insider Threat Mitigation Guide (CISA, Nov 2020, cisa.gov) — optional. The four parts, read for program elements, indicators, and privacy-and-civil-liberties constraints. Record each element RAND's SL3 measures presuppose but do not specify.
  • Secure AI Framework (Google, saif.google) — optional. The SAIF map, then the 15 risks and the controls each maps to. Grade it against RAND on one axis: does SAIF anywhere say secured against whom? Google is selling the cloud it tells you how to secure.

Do

Write the security architecture for a frontier development environment, keyed to a named security level. Choose your target SL and say why; SL3 or SL4 are the defensible ambitions. Then: (1) enumerate every path by which weights can be read, copied, or moved (training runs, checkpoint storage and backups, researcher access, inference serving, the cloud control plane, vendors), using RAND's eight urgent recommendations as the spine: the first, a security plan against a comprehensive weight-theft threat model, is this document itself, and the other seven (centralized copies, fewer authorized people, hardened access interfaces, insider program, defense-in-depth, red-teaming, confidential computing) are its section headings. (2) Attach controls per path and per attack category, honoring the report's warning that strength in one category does not compensate for weakness in another. (3) Name the two hardest-to-close vectors, each tied to a Table 5.1 category and the OC class that makes it feasible. (4) Close with the ledger: the SL this architecture achieves, the SL it cannot, and the if-then that follows, which is that a capability whose theft-consequences exceed your achievable SL is a capability this lab should not build yet. Ground that judgment in the newest card on the roster page. No build this week.

Done when the architecture states which SL it achieves and which it cannot, each residual vector names its attack category and enabling OC class checkable against Tables 5.1 and 5.2, and the final section draws the capability conclusion (what should not be built while the gap stands) rather than ending on "invest more in security."

Skip if you can already show a weight-security architecture that names its security level, its two hardest residual vectors, and the capability decision that follows.

Take away

  • Model weights are a security asset unlike documents or user data. A leaked document discloses what it says; stolen weights transfer the capability itself, minus every central control: no rate limits, no monitoring, no usage policy, no revocation.
  • "Secure" is not a property; "secure against OC3" is. RAND indexes five security levels (SL1–SL5) to five attacker operational capacities (OC1–OC5), running from a hobbyist with days and $1,000 to a top-priority state operation with years and up to $1 billion.
  • Eight of the 38 attack vectors are out of reach for OC1–OC3 yet feasible for OC4–OC5, so a lab can pass every commercial security audit and still be open to the actors that matter most. A security claim missing its OC index says nothing.
  • The authors state outright that SL5 cannot currently be achieved and will likely need national-security assistance. Ch. 7 adds that the levels "do not, in and of themselves, imply what security outcomes are required," so something outside the ladder has to supply the required outcome.
  • Insiders sit in OC3 alongside cybercrime syndicates because the investment required to defend robustly against each is comparable, even though the specific measures only partially overlap. SL3 is therefore the union of measures against that diverse group.
  • Fine-tuning strips safety training from a stolen checkpoint for under a dollar (W24's practicum, W27's Qi et al.). Theft is therefore irreversible in a way disclosure never is, and custody is the control with staying power.
  • Policy relevance: setting security requirements that scale with capability. Anthropic's Responsible Scaling Policy (v3.4, effective 8 July 2026) and DeepMind's Frontier Safety Framework (v3.1, 17 April 2026) both pair capability thresholds with required security standards for the weights. Audit them with one question: which OC class does each promised standard thwart?
  • The binding floor is EU AI Act Article 55(1)(d), administered by the Commission's AI Office: providers of GPAI models with systemic risk must ensure adequate cybersecurity protection for the model and its physical infrastructure. The GPAI Code of Practice is the compliance path; enforcement is live from August 2026.
  • Custody sits underneath every other hook, since evaluation gates, deployment rules, monitoring, incident reporting, and revocation all bite downstream of it.
Check yourself
  1. Your CISO reports "the weights are secure." What two questions does this framework oblige you to ask before that sentence means anything, and what does the existence of eight vectors infeasible for OC1–OC3 but feasible for OC4–OC5 imply about a program assembled entirely from commercial best practice?
  2. Name three specific controls that exist while weights sit behind the lab's API and vanish the moment they are stolen, then say what W24 and W27 established about the one safeguard that does travel with the checkpoint.
  3. Your lab's next training run targets a capability whose theft by a top-tier state actor you judge intolerable. Using the report's own findings about SL5, lay out the decision structure it leaves you.
  4. Why does RAND place insiders in OC3 alongside cybercrime syndicates, and what does that grouping force the SL3 benchmark to be?

Answers. (1) Secured against whom, naming the OC class, since a claim without an attacker index is unfalsifiable; and across which attack categories, since the report is explicit that investment in one category, say code execution, does not compensate for gaps in another, say physical access or human intelligence. "Secure" has to mean coverage of all nine categories at the target OC rather than excellence in one. Commercial best practice is calibrated to the attackers most companies face, OC1–OC3, so the eight OC4–OC5-exclusive vectors are state-level operations requiring defenses ordinary corporate security never installs; passing audits and being safe from states are different properties, and only the OC index distinguishes them. (2) Rate limits and usage monitoring; deployment-side classifiers and usage-policy enforcement; revocation, the power to shut access off. All are functions of the weights sitting behind an interface the lab controls. The safeguard that does travel, safety training baked into the weights, is removable by fine-tuning at trivial cost, which is why custody is the control with staying power. (3) The levels calibrate measures to expected outcomes, but Ch. 7 says the levels do not themselves imply what security outcomes are required, so the capability decision is the only candidate to supply one. If theft by OC5 is intolerable, the required outcome is SL5, and the report states flatly that achieving SL5 is currently not possible, that protecting models interacting with the internet against such actors is currently not feasible, and that the measures needed may take significant time (e.g., five years) and will likely require national-security assistance. Two options survive: radically restrict connectivity and operations now, paying the productivity costs the report names, or do not build the capability until security catches up. Note where the reasoning stops being RAND's, since the report ends at calibration and recommends starting on advanced measures soon; the don't-build conclusion is your inference to defend. (4) Insiders command far fewer resources than syndicates but start with preexisting access, and RAND's stated reason is that the investment required to robustly defend against each is comparable even though the specific measures only partially overlap. The SL3 benchmark therefore has to be the union of measures against that diverse group, which is why reaching it is more than an increment over SL2.
