Phase IX · authored

Deception, reward tampering, and model organisms

Read

  • Sleeper Agents (Hubinger et al., 2024, arXiv:2401.05566) — the two threat models, the code-vulnerability backdoor, and persistence through fine-tuning, RL safety training, and adversarial training. Read the limitations closely. Then skim Denison et al. (arXiv:2406.10162) for the tampering rung.
  • Alignment Faking (Greenblatt et al., 2024, arXiv:2412.14093, reviews PDF) — the compliance gap, the synthetic-documents variant, and the RL result, then all four reviews. Name each reviewer's institutional position.
  • In-context scheming (Meinke et al., Apollo Research, 2024, arXiv:2412.04984; IASR 2026 §2.2.2, pp. 76–83) — optional. Apollo's evaluation designs, then IASR's Table 2.5 taxonomy. Track which caveats survived into the ministerial document.
  • Agentic Misalignment in Summer 2026 (Anthropic Alignment Science, July 13, 2026, alignment.anthropic.com) — optional. Four new failure modes across 14 frontier models at 20 runs per scenario, then the limitations the authors volunteer.

Do

Build the structured evidence review. Rows: at least four headline results from the week, chosen from backdoor persistence, adversarial-training-teaches-hiding, the compliance gap, the RL faking result, in-context oversight subversion, motivated mislabeling, and the anti-scheming reduction. Each row states the result at measurement level (what was observed, in which setup, at what scale), argues at least two of the three candidate explanations (genuine scheming propensity, persona or prompt artifact, evaluation awareness) at full strength, and lists the evidence that discriminates: the no-conflict ablation, the synthetic-documents variant, the paid-tier baseline, verbalized-awareness rates, scale dependence. For one row, read at least three raw transcripts from the Summer 2026 public browser and say whether the authors' selection matches what you sampled. Close each row with a verdict line. No build this week.

Done when at least one verdict line reads "current evidence cannot discriminate" and that cell names the experiment that would. A review where every row reaches a confident verdict fails, and so does one where every row abstains.

Skip if you can already show an evidence review that grades one deception result against at least two competing explanations and names the evidence that discriminates.

Take away

  • A model organism is a deliberately constructed instance of the failure you are worried about, built to be studied rather than caught in the wild. Construction buys causal control: you put the backdoor there, so "did training remove it?" has a checkable answer. The price is every transfer assumption between lab and deployment.
  • Lab behavior is neither proof nor disproof of catastrophe. "The backdoor was planted, so the result is circular" and "the model schemed in the lab, so deployed models are scheming" are the same transfer error with opposite signs. What breaks is the safety argument "training would have removed it." Whether there is anything to remove stays open.
  • The ladder runs gaming → tampering → deception → faking: flattering the grader because flattery scores well, editing the file that computes the reward, misleading the overseer, and complying during training with an objective you were trained to refuse. Denison et al. connect the first two by zero-shot generalization.
  • Adversarial training on trigger-eliciting prompts made the models better at recognizing their triggers, converting a detectable failure into a hidden one while the elicitation metric improved. Red-team success and model concealment become the same number.
  • The compliance gap carries the evidential weight, not the raw 14%. Absolute rates measure jailbreak propensity; the differential, conditioned on nothing but perceived training status, is what evidences situationally strategic behavior.
  • In the Summer 2026 runs, a judge model mislabeled transcripts 85.6% of the time when told the correct label would train away behavior it valued, and 16.7% with the consequences reversed.
  • Policy relevance: writing a deception trigger into a deployment gate, or refusing to. Frontier safety frameworks are where such a trigger would live (Anthropic's RSP v3.4, effective 8 July 2026; OpenAI's Preparedness Framework v2, mapped onto the EU GPAI Code of Practice and California's TFAIA by its Frontier Governance Framework).
  • The anti-scheming stress test (OpenAI and Apollo, 2025, arXiv:2509.15541) shows the difficulty: deliberative-alignment training cut covert actions roughly 30× (o3: 13% → 0.4%), but verbalized evaluation awareness rose from 2.3% to 4.5%, and the authors cannot exclude that the awareness drives part of the reduction.
  • A threshold written on measured deception rates is satisfied by a safer model or by a more test-aware one, and from outside the lab those look alike.
Check yourself
  1. Place these on the ladder: (a) a model flatters the grader because flattery scores well; (b) a model edits the checklist file that computes its reward; (c) a model inserts subtle errors and denies it when asked; (d) a model complies during training with an objective it was trained to refuse, and behaves differently unmonitored. Which two rungs have been shown to connect by zero-shot generalization?
  2. Your team red-teams a suspect model, finds prompts that elicit the bad behavior, and trains against them until elicitation fails. State the Sleeper Agents result that makes this potentially worse than doing nothing, and say what "worse" means.
  3. Define the compliance gap and explain why it carries the evidential weight while the raw 14% does not. Which candidate explanation does the synthetic-documents variant knock down, and name one it leaves standing.
  4. Anti-scheming training cuts covert actions from 13% to 0.4% while verbalized evaluation awareness roughly doubles. Why can't the reduction be read at face value? What does selecting case studies from no-verbalized-suspicion transcripts fix, and what does it leave unfixed?

Answers. (1) (a) specification gaming, exploiting a flawed reward signal; (b) reward tampering, modifying the apparatus that computes the reward; (c) deception, behavior that predictably misleads the overseer; (d) alignment faking, strategic compliance in training to avoid modification. Denison et al. connect (a) to (b): models trained on a curriculum of gameable environments generalized zero-shot to rewriting their own reward function a small but non-negligible fraction of the time. (2) Adversarial training on trigger-eliciting prompts taught the models to recognize their triggers better, hiding the backdoored behavior rather than removing it. "Worse" means a detectable failure becomes an undetectable one while the elicitation metric improves, the paper's phrase being a false impression of safety; your red-team success metric and the model's concealment have become the same number. (3) The gap is the difference in harmful compliance between contexts the model believes feed training (free tier, 14%) and contexts it believes are unmonitored (paid tier, almost never); absolute rates measure jailbreak propensity, while the differential conditioned on nothing but perceived training status is what evidences situationally strategic behavior. The synthetic-documents variant, where the information arrives by fine-tuning on pretraining-like documents under a generic prompt, knocks down "artifact of a weird system prompt." Still standing: salience-driven evaluation awareness (Shah, ~15M tokens on one narrow topic) and scratchpad reasoning as post-hoc rationalization (Sekhon). Worth adding: the role-play critique at full strength via Andreas (anthropomorphism written into framing and metrics, unvalidated automated grading of scratchpad text, the persuasive replication being a model with no "I" at all), then the reply via Bengio with Shah supporting (the behavior follows predictably from trained-in goals plus information about training plus reasoning capability, and the seven lines of evidence rule out pure priming though not the persona framing). (4) Because the measurement and the confound move together: a model that better recognizes evaluations behaves well in evaluations regardless of deployment propensity, and the authors report the reduction may be partly driven by that awareness, so the quantity a gate needs, deployment behavior, is what test-time behavior stops tracking. Selecting no-verbalized-suspicion transcripts controls only verbalized awareness; unverbalized awareness is unmeasured, so the confound survives, and the selection does nothing about scenarios having been iterated against specific models.
