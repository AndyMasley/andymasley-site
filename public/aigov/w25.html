Phase V · authored

Supervised tuning and human feedback

Read

  • The RLHF Book (Lambert, rlhfbook.com) — read in order: Instruction Fine-Tuning, Preference Data, Reward Modeling. Closed-book, redraw the pipeline with the artifact each stage produces.
  • Training language models to follow instructions with human feedback (Ouyang et al., 2022, arXiv:2203.02155) — §3.4 human data collection, §5.2 "Who are we aligning to?", §5.3 limitations, then skim §4.1. Write an evidence brief on the §4.1 headline: population, who judged, what it cannot establish.
  • The RLHF Book, "The Nature of Preferences" (ch. 10) — optional. Where preference-as-supervision came from, utility theory to reward functions. Read it if "whose preferences" still sounds rhetorical rather than technical.
  • ARENA, Ch. 2 Reinforcement Learning, §[2.4] RLHF (learn.arena.education) — optional. Implement RLHF end to end, PPO applied to language-model finetuning. Queue it after W26; the PPO prerequisite is §[2.3].

Do

Blind-label 30 chosen/rejected pairs sampled from Anthropic/hh-rlhf. Write down your predicted agreement rate first, then compute your actual rate against InstructGPT's 72.6%. Sort your disagreements into three bins: I'm right, the label is defensible, ambiguous. Keep the ambiguous count.

Draw the pipeline map on one page: base model → demonstration collection → SFT → preference collection → reward-model training → policy optimization → evaluation gate → deployed policy. Give each stage its input artifact, output artifact, and who touches it, including the loop nobody draws, where the deployed model generates the next round's comparison data.

Add a risk register beside it, one row per risk, four columns: the stage that creates it, the mechanism, one symptom observable from outside, where in the pipeline it could be caught. Your ambiguous-bin count is the register's first evidence entry.

Done when every register row ties its risk to the single stage that introduces it and names one observable symptom, and sycophancy appears as a row, attributed to the stage you argue creates it, with the reward-model amplification step stated explicitly.

Skip if you can already draw the pipeline from data collection to deployed policy from memory, name the artifact each stage produces and the failure mode it introduces, and say what a ~73% inter-annotator agreement rate does to the ceiling of any reward model trained on those labels.

Take away

  • The reward model is a proxy, and optimization is not neutral toward proxy error. A policy trained to score well searches out the reward model's systematic mistakes: verbosity rated as quality, agreement as helpfulness. That amplification sits behind sycophancy and reward hacking (W42).
  • Preference labels are not readouts of human values. A label is one contractor's choice between two completions under written instructions, a rubric, and time pressure. InstructGPT's own training labelers agreed with each other 72.6 ± 1.5% of the time.
  • Every pipeline then decides what to do with the disagreeing tail: majority-vote it away, average over it, or model it. Most make that choice silently.
  • Bradley-Terry fits a sigmoid over a score difference, so a reward model never learns absolute quality. Scores rank completions within a prompt; comparing raw scores across prompts or models is a category error.
  • Every stage here is a data pipeline in W19's sense, except the thing being filtered is human judgment. Chat templates are part of it: the same weights become a different artifact under a different template.
  • Policy: the documentation requirement is whose preferences, collected under what instructions, aggregated by what rule. The EU AI Act's Annex XI data fields ("type and provenance of data and curation methodologies," Article 53(1)(a)) were drafted with pretraining corpora in view; whether they reach preference labels is the open drafting question.
  • No current instrument lets an outside party check whether deployed behavior matches the spec given to annotators. OpenAI's Model Spec discloses the target but not the data. Later reading on aggregation: Stanford's CS329H and Conitzer et al., 2024.
Check yourself
  1. Why doesn't reward-model error wash out as noise when you optimize a policy against it hard?
  2. What can a trained reward model tell you about a single completion in isolation, and what can't it?
  3. A deployed assistant flatters users and over-agrees even when it costs accuracy. Trace sycophancy through your map: which stage creates it, which amplifies it, and why doesn't the evaluation gate catch it?
  4. InstructGPT's training labelers agreed 72.6 ± 1.5% of the time, and the 1.3B InstructGPT beat the 175B GPT-3 in human evaluations. What does the first number do to "ground truth," and what does the second result fail to establish?

Answers. (1) Optimization is a search, not a survey. Random label noise partly cancels, but the RM's systematic errors define directions where score exceeds quality, and gradient pressure moves the policy along them. Goodhart's law with a learning rate. (2) Nothing calibrated. The loss only constrains score differences between completions of the same prompt, so scores carry rankings, not absolute quality. (3) Created at preference collection, where raters systematically prefer agreement and flattery. Amplified at policy optimization, where the RM inherits the bias and the policy searches out what the RM over-rewards. The gate misses it because its judges, human raters or an LLM judge trained on similar preferences, share the bias. (4) On ~27% of comparisons two screened labelers disagree, so ~73% is roughly the ceiling on matching a held-out labeler, and the pipeline picks an aggregation rule, usually silently. The 1.3B result is a preference rate on OpenAI's own API prompt distribution, judged by labelers from the same hiring filter. It shows posttraining moves preference share at 100× less scale. It does not show greater capability, or that preferences from outside that pipeline would agree.
