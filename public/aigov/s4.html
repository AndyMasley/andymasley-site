Compute governance

Read

  • Sastry, Heim, Belfield et al., "Computing Power and the Governance of Artificial Intelligence" (arXiv:2402.08797) — the four governable properties and what they license, then the limitations and risks sections. Write your own defeater list, with a credence that each bites by 2030, before the limitations section.
  • Heim, Fist, Egan, Huang et al., "Governing Through the Cloud" (governance.ai) — providers as securers, record keepers, verifiers, and enforcers, and the claim that workload type and compute consumed are readable from non-confidential information. Write an evidence brief on that classification claim.
  • Petrie, Aarne, Ammann, Dalrymple, "Flexible Hardware-Enabled Guarantees for AI Compute" (arXiv:2506.15093) — part one of a series, with no deployment timeline offered. For each guarantee, name the physical or institutional assumption it stands on, then sort every claim into buildable-by-2030 or aspiration.
  • Wasil, Reed, Miller, Barnett, "Verification methods for international AI agreements" (arXiv:2408.16074) — optional, the S6 bridge. Ten methods in three families: national technical means, access-dependent, hardware-dependent. Read for the taxonomy and hold the treaty mechanics for S6.

Do

Build the compute-lever map, one row per lever, at least eight, spanning the stack: training-compute thresholds, large-run reporting, cloud KYC, chip export controls (the ECCN layer), upstream supply-chain controls on fabrication and tooling, outbound investment screening, cloud-provider enforcement, HEMs, location verification, international verification regimes.

Columns: mechanism, the named instrument and authority (R7 supplies these); current status, dated, citing pages/sources.md; failure modes, technical decay, evasion, political; matrix signs for your six priority dossiers (fast-takeoff, scheming, misuse-bio, coups, great-power war, safety-tax collapse) as sign/conditional/crux; decay parameter.

All forty-eight-plus signs live in the map, so promote only the contested few into matrix/matrix.md, which keeps its ~50-cell cap.

The export-control saga runs through the week as the evidence stream, from October 2022's first advanced-computing rule through the January 2026 case-by-case licensing regime for H200-class chips. It is one of the eight case-block cases, so write your memo before reading its sealed retrospectives.

Then draft the flagship skeleton: all five headings of flagship/candidate-1.md, the question stated decision-shaped in one sentence, at least three numbers carried over from the map. Write this memo even if your month-three flagship pick landed on candidate 2 or 3.

Done when the map covers at least eight levers and every row's final column names the single world-model parameter that most threatens that lever, with all three of W20's decay mechanisms (algorithmic progress, distillation, inference-time scaling) among the named parameters, each carrying a number: a rate, a halving time, or a dated threshold crossing. A map where every lever fears the same parameter fails.

Skip if you already have a map to this spec: eight-plus levers with mechanism, dated status, failure modes, matrix signs, and a named decay parameter each. Refresh the statuses against the source register and move to S5.

Take away

  • Compute governance works because compute is detectable, excludable, quantifiable, and produced by a concentrated supply chain. Those four properties are conditional on the scaling paradigm holding, not laws of nature, and each lever cashes one or more of them.
  • The levers are ordered: HEMs enable verification, verification enables treaties, treaties enable any credible slowdown. Dismissing hardware mechanisms as exotic quietly deletes the top of that chain.
  • Export controls are not AI safety policy by default. The flagship US "AI safety" lever serves great-power-competition threat models. For loss-of-control risk from the leading domestic labs it does nothing, and may worsen things by hardening the race framing that spends the safety tax.
  • A map sign is a conditional, not a verdict. Export controls score + for great-power war only if they slow the rival more than they accelerate indigenization, so the H200 memo can land either way. The crux is the good-enough-stack parameter: rival capability's elasticity to marginal chip access, a number nobody has published carefully.
Check yourself
  1. Name the four governable properties, and for each give one lever that cashes it and one post-2024 development that erodes it.
  2. A senator calls export controls "the single most important AI safety policy." Under which threat models is that defensible, under which does the sign go to zero or negative, and what single world-model assumption flips it?
  3. What two things does the cloud paper claim a provider can read from non-confidential information it already holds, which post-2024 training practice blurs the classification, and what does that do to a KYC trigger written on "training runs above X FLOP"?
  4. The dependency chain runs HEMs → verification → treaties → slowdown. Why that direction, and which FlexHEG component failing collapses which of Wasil's three verification families back into which other one?

Answers. (1) Detectable, cashed by reporting and thresholds (W21: a training run is five numbers you can rebuild from public information); eroded by decentralized training splitting runs below any single site's visibility. Excludable, cashed by export controls and KYC; eroded by the maturing good-enough domestic stack and by open weights, which leave exclusion with no object once the artifact diffuses. Quantifiable, cashed by FLOP-denominated triggers; eroded by inference-time scaling, which buys capability in a unit the metric never sees. Concentrated, cashed by chokepoint controls on fabrication, tooling, and cloud; eroded by indigenization. (2) Defensible under great-power war and competition models, and under safety-tax collapse via the lead-as-safety-budget argument, since a compute lead is the slack that makes a domestic slowdown affordable. Zero-to-negative under fast-takeoff and scheming from the leading domestic labs: controls do nothing about the frontrunner's own run, and under MAIM assumptions they raise a rival's incentive to preempt. The flipping assumption is rival perception. (3) Workload type (training versus inference) and compute consumption. W28's regime blurs it: verifiable-reward RL and test-time scaling spend inference-shaped compute that builds capability, so a "training run above X FLOP" trigger both under-triggers and over-triggers. Trigger design migrates toward aggregate-consumption and conduct-based tests, the direction the cloud paper's record-keeper and verifier roles anticipate. (4) Treaties need verification you can trust without trusting the counterparty. National technical means are weak against data centers that look like warehouses, and access-dependent inspection is invasive enough that states refuse it, so hardware-dependent methods are the only family offering per-chip granularity with privacy preserved. FlexHEG's two components are the guarantee processor and the tamper-protecting enclosure; if the enclosure fails, every attestation the processor signs becomes forgeable and the hardware-dependent family collapses back into access-dependent inspection.
