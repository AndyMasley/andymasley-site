Phase I · authored

Automatic differentiation

Read

  • Karpathy, "The spelled-out intro to neural networks and backpropagation" (karpathy.ai/zero-to-hero) — core, ~2.5 hours of video: the scalar Value object through the multilayer perceptron. Code alongside, pausing to predict each derivative before the backward step.
  • Parr & Howard, "The Matrix Calculus You Need for Deep Learning" (arXiv:1802.01528) — core: Sec. 3 partial derivatives, 4.1 the Jacobian, 4.5 the chain rules, 5 gradient of neuron activation, 6 gradient of the neural network loss. Rework the displayed examples closed-book with dimensions beside every factor.
  • PyTorch, "Automatic Differentiation with torch.autograd" (tutorial) — core, a sitting. Inspect .grad at every leaf of a small computation and check the numbers against your own engine.

Do

Build a scalar autograd engine: a Value class with +, *, **, and one nonlinearity, a topological-sort backward(), and gradient accumulation at shared nodes, plus a test file and a half-page mechanism note (three sentences, no jargon lost, no mechanism lost).

Then break it deliberately. Swap gradient accumulation (+=) for plain assignment (=) in your backward pass, rerun a function that reuses a subexpression, and explain which path's contribution vanished.

Done when your engine's gradients match central finite-difference numerical gradients to within 1e-6 on three test functions, at least one composing your nonlinearity and at least one reusing a shared subexpression, the same Value feeding two paths. Know why the bar is 1e-6 and not 1e-12 before you claim a pass.

Skip if you can already differentiate a two-layer scalar computation by hand, label every local derivative, and verify the result numerically (gateway check 7).

Take away

  • Reverse-mode differentiation answers one question in one backward sweep: how does this single scalar loss change with respect to everything upstream. The forward pass stores each node's local derivative; the backward pass multiplies them along every path and adds where paths merge.
  • Cost is a small constant multiple of the forward pass whether the model has ten parameters or a trillion. That is what makes training deep networks feasible at all.
  • Autodiff is not costless symbolic differentiation. It never builds a derivative formula. It computes exact derivatives of the program you actually ran, and it pays in memory: every intermediate activation stays alive until its backward step needs it.
  • That is why training takes several times the memory of inference, why activation checkpointing exists (W22), and why "just fine-tune it on a laptop" usually fails as a memory claim before it fails as a FLOPs claim.
  • This mechanism sits under every training claim downstream: W8's forward and backward propagation, W9's vanishing and exploding gradients, W13's attention derivatives, W15's GPT training loop.
  • Decision: evaluate claims about training mechanism, compute, and reproducibility. Compute-threshold regimes regulate a number someone has to count: EU AI Act Article 51's 10²⁵-FLOP presumption, with the AI Office writing the measurement guidance, and SB 53's 10²⁶-FLOP test.
  • The standard estimate of ~6 FLOPs per parameter per training token exists because the backward pass costs roughly twice the forward pass, a fact about this mechanism rather than a convention.
  • Whoever drafts the counting rules decides whether backward-pass FLOPs, discarded runs, and fine-tuning count toward the threshold, and each choice moves real models across the line.
Check yourself
  1. One Value in your graph feeds two downstream operations. What must its gradient equal after backward(), which rule of calculus says so, and what symptom does = instead of += produce?
  2. Why does reverse mode deliver every parameter's gradient for a small constant multiple of one forward pass, while forward mode would need one pass per parameter, and what resource does reverse mode pay in instead?
  3. For g: ℝⁿ→ℝᵐ, f: ℝᵐ→ℝ, and L = f(g(x)): write the shape of each Jacobian in the vector chain rule and the shape of their product. What object is that product?
  4. Your finite-difference check disagrees with your engine in the fourth decimal place. Which one is exact, why, and what two error sources afflict the other?

Answers. (1) The sum of the gradients arriving along each path, by the multivariable chain rule (total derivative). With =, whichever branch the topological order visits last overwrites the other's contribution, so gradients go silently wrong when and only when subexpressions are shared, the bug the third test function exists to catch. (2) Reverse mode propagates one scalar's sensitivity backward, so each node computes ∂L/∂(itself) once, reusing stored local derivatives, and the number of inputs never enters the cost. Forward mode carries one directional derivative per pass: a million parameters, a million passes. The bill is memory, because every activation a local derivative needs stays live until its backward step. (3) ∂f/∂g is 1×m, ∂g/∂x is m×n, and the product is 1×n, the gradient ∇L as a row vector over the inputs. (4) The engine is exact to floating point, since it multiplies exact local derivatives of the operations actually executed. Finite differences carry truncation error, O(h²) for central differences, plus cancellation when subtracting nearly equal numbers; shrink h too far and roundoff grows. That trade-off is why the acceptance bar is 1e-6.
