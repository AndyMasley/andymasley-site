Reinforcement learning foundations

Read

  • Spinning Up in Deep RL (Achiam, OpenAI, spinningup.openai.com) — Parts 1–3: key concepts, the algorithm taxonomy, intro to policy optimization. Effectively frozen since 2020, so the code targets the pre-Gymnasium gym API. Next day, re-derive the simplest policy gradient closed-book.
  • RLHF Book, Ch. 6 "Reinforcement Learning" (Lambert, rlhfbook.com/c/06-policy-gradients) — the role of RL in RLHF, "Policy Gradient Algorithms" through "Value Functions and PPO," and "Double Regularization." Stop before GRPO; W28 picks it up. Source the objective r = r_θ − β·D_KL now.
  • Sutton & Barto, Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction, 2nd ed. (full text) — optional reference. Ch. 3 Finite Markov Decision Processes when the MDP formalism goes by too fast, Ch. 13 Policy Gradient Methods for the theorem with proofs. Reach for it on demand.

Do

Port Spinning Up's simplest policy gradient (1_simple_pg.py, 128 lines of PyTorch) to Gymnasium and train it on CartPole-v1 (+1 per surviving step, truncated at 500). The port is small: step now returns five values with a terminated/truncated split, and reset returns (obs, info). Add reward-to-go, run at least three seeds, log per-episode return, and measure the random-policy baseline rather than assuming it.

Then swap in a subtly different reward that pays +1 only while the cart sits in the right half of the track. Write down the behavior you expect, retrain, and watch what the optimizer finds.

Write it up in one page plus one figure: environment and algorithm named, config and seeds stated, learning curves across seeds with the measured baseline marked, the perturbed-reward result with predicted versus observed behavior, and a closing paragraph connecting proxy error to learned behavior.

Done when the figure shows mean episode return across at least three seeds with per-seed curves or a spread band, the random-policy baseline measured over the same number of episodes, and the final mean clearing that baseline by more than the seed-to-seed spread.

Skip if you can already write the policy-gradient estimator and the RLHF objective r = r_θ − β·D_KL from memory with every symbol named, and say when PPO's clipping does nothing at all.

Take away

  • A policy gradient is bookkeeping: sample actions, score outcomes, shift parameters so well-scored actions become more probable. The estimator weights ∇log π(a|s) by return or advantage. Everything else is control machinery for that one estimator.
  • Baselines and advantages cut the estimator's variance without touching its expectation. PPO's clipping and the KL penalty leash how far one batch can drag the policy.
  • The policy is not the reward function. The policy is the model you deploy; the reward is training-time scaffolding that no longer exists at deployment. Behavior is wherever optimization landed while chasing the proxy's score, errors included. "Trained to be helpful" is a claim about a training signal.
  • Map RLHF onto the MDP: state is prompt plus tokens so far, action is the next token, policy is the language model, and reward arrives once at the end of the completion. That sparse, delayed signal over hundreds of token-actions is why baselines, value functions, and advantages exist.
  • Under one gradient step per batch, a common LM setting, PPO's importance ratios never leave the clip interval. Clipping never activates, the KL penalty does all the leashing, and PPO reduces to REINFORCE. Whether PPO is doing anything is a question about your batch schedule.
  • Policy: what a posttraining disclosure regime should demand about reward specification. Article 53(1)(a) and Annex XI oblige GPAI providers to document "training methodologies and techniques, the key design choices including the rationale and assumptions made," administered by the Commission's AI Office.
  • The reward specification is the highest-leverage line that documentation could carry, since what you optimize you get. A reviewer who can ask "what was the reward, what was β, how many gradient steps per batch" is asking whether the leash was on. W27 and W42 decide how much weight the line can bear.
Check yourself
  1. What is a policy gradient estimating, and why does the estimator multiply ∇log π(a|s) by a return or advantage rather than differentiating the reward directly?
  2. Subtracting a baseline b(s) changes nothing about the gradient's expectation. What does it change, and what symptom appears in your build if you skip it?
  3. In r = r_θ − β·D_KL, name each term's job and describe the trained model at β = 0 and as β → ∞.
  4. Why does PPO exist at all if REINFORCE is the same estimator?

Answers. (1) The gradient of expected return with respect to policy parameters. The environment's reward isn't differentiable through the sampling, so the log-derivative trick converts "differentiate what the world did" into "reweight what the policy said," each log-prob gradient weighted by how good the outcome was. The update touches the policy; the reward only supplies the weights. (2) Variance only. b(s) doesn't depend on the action, so its contribution has zero expectation, but it recenters the weights so actions are pushed up only when they beat expectations. Skip it and every action in a good episode gets reinforced, bad ones included: the estimator still converges in expectation, but seed spread can swallow the learning curve. (3) r_θ is the reward model's score, the learned proxy being maximized; β·D_KL penalizes divergence from the frozen reference policy. At β = 0 the policy is free to drift wherever the reward model misjudges, giving reward hacking and degenerate completions. As β → ∞ the model collapses back to the reference, perfectly safe and perfectly useless. (4) Clipping caps how far one batch's importance ratios can drag the policy, which is step-size control for taking multiple gradient steps on the same samples. Under one step per batch the ratios never leave the clip interval, so PPO reduces to REINFORCE at those hyperparameters.
