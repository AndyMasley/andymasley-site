R9: Comparative and international governance

Read

  • Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, the AI Act (EUR-Lex) — prohibited practices, high-risk systems, general-purpose models, governance, enforcement, transition. Cite articles, not summaries.
  • Interim Measures for the Management of Generative AI Services (CAC, official text) — plus the algorithm recommendation, deep synthesis, labeling, filing, and ethics review measures. For each, write down the obligated party and the trigger.
  • Hiroshima AI Process reporting framework (OECD, portal) — version 2.0 and the 25 first-round reports, submitted at transparency.oecd.ai; next reports due 1 September 2026. List what version 2.0 changed.

Do

The case. Read three verification regimes from their annexes. New START: 18 on-site inspections a year, ten Type One and eight Type Two, unique identifiers on delivery vehicles, telemetry on up to five launches, a Bilateral Consultative Commission for disputes, and expiry on 5 February 2026 with no successor. IAEA safeguards: INFCIRC/153 accounts for declared material at declared facilities, INFCIRC/540 is opt-in for the rest. The BWC: a protocol negotiated from 1995 to 2001 and abandoned. One hour, half a page: which properties transfer to a compute-verification regime, which break, and on which disanalogy. Then read Baker (arXiv:2304.04123) and mark where your list differs.

Build a common-field comparison across the EU, China, the UK, Japan, Korea, and the US. Fields: regulatory object, legal authority, risk classification, registration or filing, testing duties, data duties, content controls, enforcement and penalties, industrial policy, security integration, international strategy. Three jurisdictions come with assigned texts; find the other three yourself (the UK has no cross-cutting AI statute, Japan's 2025 promotion act carries no penalties, Korea's AI Framework Act took effect 22 January 2026).

Then write the negotiation brief for a delegation you are staffing: one coordination mechanism that could bind, one irreducible conflict, red lines, and fallback positions.

Done when every cell cites a primary legal or official text with its current status, no jurisdiction is described in caricature, and each difference is explained by an institution or an incentive rather than by national temperament.

Take away

  • The regimes regulate different objects, so "stricter" is usually a category error. The EU regulates products placed on its market and tiers them by risk. China regulates services offered to the public inside China. The US works through sectoral agencies, procurement, and export controls.
  • The UK and Japan add no cross-cutting obligations and lean on existing regulators.
  • Enforcement leverage differs more than text does. The AI Act reaches 7% of worldwide turnover or €35M for prohibited practices and 3% or €15M for most provider obligations, with general-purpose model duties enforced centrally by the AI Office.
  • China's leverage is the filing and security-assessment regime plus the power to pull a service. Washington's is contract terms, agency authority, and licenses.
  • China's stack is layered: the Interim Measures (effective 15 August 2023) over the algorithm recommendation provisions (2022), deep synthesis provisions (2023), and synthetic-content labeling measures (effective 1 September 2025). Article 4's core socialist values requirement is the organizing purpose.
  • Voluntary international frameworks bind nothing and are still worth the hours. HAIP reports are self-drafted and unverified, and were barely comparable across firms until version 2.0 forced closed-ended questions. What they create is a dated public record you can quote back at a company.
  • Policy relevance: coordination binds where sovereignty costs are low and technical content is shared. Feasible: incident-report formats, evaluation methods, mutual recognition of test results. Not feasible: content rules rooted in incompatible speech regimes, or inspection of a rival's training runs. S6 has the verification mechanics.
Check yourself
  1. A Chinese firm trains a model and uses it internally, never offering it to the public. Which instruments bite, and why not the Interim Measures?
  2. Under the AI Act, who enforces general-purpose model obligations, and what is the ceiling for a prohibited practice?
  3. Name one coordination mechanism that could bind all six jurisdictions and one conflict that cannot be negotiated away.
  4. Why is a HAIP transparency report weak evidence of compliance and still useful to you?

Answers. (1) The Interim Measures cover generative AI services provided to the public within China, so internal or non-public use falls outside them; the firm is still reached by PIPL, DSL, CSL, and science-and-technology ethics review, and any public release would trigger filing, security assessment, and labeling. (2) The AI Office within the Commission for general-purpose models, national market surveillance authorities for the rest; prohibited practices reach €35M or 7% of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher. (3) Feasible: a common serious-incident report format and mutual recognition of test methods through the safety-institute network, both technical and low-cost to sovereignty. Irreducible: content requirements, where the Chinese values mandate and EU fundamental-rights law cannot both be satisfied by one product configuration. (4) It is self-reported, unverified, and carries no penalty for omission. It is also dated, public, and quotable, which makes it a commitment record and a baseline for drafting binding text.
