Phase VIII · authored

Capability trends and forecasting

Read

  • **Tetlock & Gardner, *Superforecasting: The Art and Science of Prediction*** (Crown, 2015) — Ch. 3 "Keeping Score" and Ch. 5's outside-view discipline. Score three hypothetical forecasts by hand using (p − outcome)², the convention Metaculus and your log use; Tetlock's 0–2 scale doubles every number.
  • Epoch AI trends dashboards (epoch.ai/trends) — frontier training compute (~5×/year since 2020, 90% CI 4–6×) and the Epoch Capabilities Index (~15.5 ECI points/year since early 2024), then the ECI documentation. Read the methodology and uncertainty notes, not the headlines.
  • AI Index Report 2026, Ch. 2 Technical Performance (Stanford HAI, hai.stanford.edu/ai-index/2026-ai-index-report) — the trend charts, and beneath each the source note: whose data, which benchmark, what the axis is. Any chart you would cite enters your claims file with source, scope, and date.
  • METR time horizons, revisited (metr.org/time-horizons) — a short return, not a reread; W32's caveats all still apply. Take the trend as a forecasting input: 207-day doubling (95% bootstrap CI 166–240), recent points above the fit. Write one horizon forecast under three branches (continues, reverts, breaks) with a probability on each.

Do

The forecast log is the build. Add three new questions to forecasts/log.md in the repo, each resolving inside 12 months, each with the full rationale the format demands: reference class, base rate inside it, inside-view adjustment with direction and size, bias direction. Draw one from an AI Index Ch. 2 chart, one from Epoch's dashboards, and one from the METR trend. Where the question exists on Metaculus, link it and record the community median. Respect the log's two rules: the cap of 25 active questions, and the counting rule that Brier-versus-median comparisons are calibration hygiene rather than skill until 40-plus resolutions. For one question, write the probability the bare trend extrapolation implies before writing your own number.

Take one of the three to full standard: resolution criteria a stranger could adjudicate, resolution date inside 12 months, reference class and base rate stated, inside-view adjustment and bias direction written, final probability signed. Add a half-page "what would change my number" note listing the observables you will watch, each with a direction and rough size.

Done when the logged forecast resolves within 12 months by its own written criteria, so a reader who is not you could score it on resolution day without asking you anything, and names its reference class; and the note names two observables, each with the direction it moves your number.

Skip if your log already carries three or more sub-12-month questions with named reference classes and base rates, plus one resolved entry you scored.

Take away

  • Every forecast is a base rate plus an adjustment. Outside view first: Fermi-ize, find the reference class, take its base rate as the starting number, then let the inside view move it, writing down which direction and why.
  • Brier closes the loop by making honest probabilities the optimal strategy, so resolution criteria are non-negotiable. A forecast that cannot be scored cannot be wrong, and cannot be evidence about anything, including your own calibration.
  • One trend line cannot carry capability, impact, and policy. A capability forecast extended into an impact claim silently adds adoption, complements, workflow integration, and institutions. An impact claim extended into a recommendation silently adds values, alternatives, authority, and costs.
  • The three instruments have different weaknesses. Compute curves are the most observable (~5×/year, 90% CI 4–6×). The ECI spans saturation by chaining benchmarks of different difficulties, but its scale is a relative latent construct with no absolute meaning, it cannot tell you the composition of gains, and it inherits every component benchmark's contamination and decay.
  • METR's doubling is a fitted parameter with an interval: 207 days, 95% bootstrap CI 166–240, recent points above the fit. Carry W32's caveats (suite-bound, clean software tasks, fitted threshold) with the number every time you use it.
  • Policy: the live case is whether trend extrapolation justifies acting before harm arrives. The International AI Safety Report 2026 carries the ~2-hour horizon and its ~7-month doubling into a record some thirty governments commissioned, and every briefing citing it is implicitly arguing the trend will hold.
  • Give that argument its structure. It is a forecast, not a measurement, and it rests on a reference class of clean software tasks fitted 2019–2025. The case for early action is decision-theoretic: acting too early wastes authority on a trend that breaks, acting too late means the capability arrives inside institutions that cannot respond at trend speed.
  • Writing the extrapolation as a scored forecast with resolution criteria and update triggers gives the recommended regime a built-in check.
Check yourself
  1. A briefing shows the ECI rising ~15 points/year and concludes: "AI will displace a large share of paralegal work by 2028, so bar associations should impose supervision rules now." Walk the claim ladder and name what goes unargued at each crossing.
  2. Your log shows 0.8 on an event that failed to happen. Compute the Brier score. A colleague advises putting 0.5 on everything so you never look bad. Refute with the expected-score argument, and say what the log's counting rule makes of your score either way.
  3. What does the ECI's stitching let it do that no single benchmark can, and name two things the same methodology makes it unable to tell you.
  4. "Will an agent complete, at 50% reliability, a task that takes humans a full work week, within 24 months?" Build the outside view from this week's numbers before touching any inside view. Show the arithmetic and where the CI leaves you.

Answers. (1) The measurement is a latent-scale composite of benchmark scores. First crossing, to a capability forecast: "the trend continues" needs a reference-class argument, not a ruler. Second, to an impact forecast: displacement adds adoption, workflow integration, complements, error tolerance, and institutional friction, none of them measured by any benchmark. Third, to a recommendation: values, alternatives, authority, costs. (2) Brier = (0.8 − 0)² = 0.64. Always-0.5 scores 0.25 on everything, but Brier is proper: if your true belief is 0.8, reporting it gives expected score 0.8(0.04) + 0.2(0.64) = 0.16 against 0.25 for reporting 0.5. Hedging beats you only where the true frequency sits closer to 0.5 than your stated number, which is a calibration failure rather than a strategy. Per the log's counting rule, before ~40 resolutions any Brier comparison is calibration hygiene, so one bad score is a data point. (3) Stitching spans saturation, comparing systems across eras no single benchmark survives. It cannot tell you what any score means in absolute terms, since the scale is a relative latent construct and "15 points/year" has no unit outside the model, nor the composition of gains (general capability versus a few domains moving). It launders out none of the component contamination and decay. (4) A work week ≈ 40 hours; anchor on the ~2-hour 50% horizon the ministerial record carries, so you need log₂(40/2) ≈ 4.3 doublings. At 207 days per doubling that is ~890 days ≈ 29 months, outside the window; at the CI's fast edge of 166 days, ~715 days ≈ 23.5 months, just inside. The outside view puts the event near but mostly past the deadline, so a starting probability well under half, before adjusting up for the above-trend recent points or down for the suite's clean-task external-validity problem.
