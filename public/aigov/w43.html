Phase IX · authored

Control and monitoring

Read

  • AI Control: Improving Safety Despite Intentional Subversion (Greenblatt, Shlegeris, Sachan & Roger, 2023, arXiv:2312.06942) — the setting, the protocol family (trusted monitoring, defer to trusted, trusted editing, untrusted monitoring), and the evaluation methodology. Rebuild the evaluation loop from memory.
  • The case for ensuring that powerful AIs are controlled (Shlegeris & Greenblatt, Redwood Research blog, May 2024, blog.redwoodresearch.org) — the useful-but-catchable window and the two definitions doing the work. Name the crux that would empty the window.
  • Safety Cases: How to Justify the Safety of Advanced AI Systems (Clymer, Gabrieli, Krueger & Larsen, 2024, arXiv:2403.10462) — optional. The control argument type only, among the paper's four (inability, control, trustworthiness, deference). Keep a ledger entry per type recording the evidence each demands.
  • Ten People on the Inside (Shlegeris, Redwood Research blog, Jan 2025, blog.redwoodresearch.org) — optional. The minimal-political-will limit case. Control's chief advocate argues that control is what survives minimal buy-in; say whether that institutional position strengthens or discounts the claim.

Do

Write a control protocol plus red-team plan for a named internal deployment: an AI R&D assistant with read–write access to your training and evaluation repositories, drafting experiment code, proposing PRs, and triaging results. The protocol specifies which model is trusted and on what grounds, what the untrusted assistant may touch, the monitoring layers with who audits what fraction, and the escalation path when something is flagged. The red-team plan enumerates the protocol's trust assumptions, ranks them by weakness, and scripts a concrete attack on the weakest. Close with the cost accounting: if the scripted attack had succeeded, what does the attacker now have? Protocol design is the build; running a full control evaluation against W40's toolchain is a project, not a sitting.

Done when the protocol states its trust assumptions as an explicit list, the red-team plan's lead attack targets the assumption you ranked weakest rather than the one easiest to write about, and the cost accounting prices a successful attack in concrete objects (what was corrupted, exfiltrated, or made possible) rather than adjectives.

Skip if you can already show a control protocol for a concrete internal deployment that lists its trust assumptions and red-teams the weakest one.

Take away

  • Control seeks acceptable safety despite an untrusted model: a weaker trusted model monitors the stronger one, authority and access are restricted, and scarce human auditing is spent on the most suspicious actions. The evidence is adversarial: the claim is "the best attack our red team could construct fails," never "the model wouldn't."
  • Alignment and control are not the same intervention. Alignment tries to make the model not intend subversion, a property of the model; control tries to make intended subversion fail, a property of the protocol around it. A briefing that treats "we red-teamed it and it behaved" as a control argument has confused the two.
  • The red team plays the untrusted model, converting an unmeasurable question (will this model attack?) into a measurable one (can any attack policy we construct beat the protocol?). A weak red team turns "safe" into "safe against people who didn't try very hard."
  • Protocols are scored on usefulness too: deferring to the weak model everywhere is safe and pointless.
  • Untrusted monitoring uses a monitor as capable as the attacker and inherits collusion: both are instances of the same untrusted model, so an attack can carry a signal telling the monitor to wave it through. The countermeasure is upfront validation on inputs known to contain backdoors.
  • The window closes as the capability gap between trusted labor (humans plus weaker trusted models) and the untrusted model grows. Past some gap, black-box control fails and the safety case falls back on the trustworthiness arguments control was designed to avoid.
  • Policy relevance: deciding what control evidence a deployment decision should demand, when nobody currently has to. California's SB 53 attaches its transparency report at deployment; its only reach inside the lab is a large-developer duty to send quarterly summaries of internal-use catastrophic-risk assessments to the California Office of Emergency Services.
  • The EU AI Act's Article 55 obligations bind providers of systemic-risk models placed on the Union market, and the lab-friendly reading of Article 2(8) treats everything before market placement as exempt research and development.
  • Apollo Research's analysis of internal deployment under the Act argues that in-house use is already "putting into service," so the exemption ends where internal deployment begins. That is a memorandum, not enforcement practice.
Check yourself
  1. A lab reports: "our alignment evaluations came back clean, so a control protocol would be redundant." Separate the two interventions, and say why evidence for one is not evidence for the other.
  2. The untrusted model's role during evaluation is played by a red-team attack policy rather than the model itself. What does that substitution buy methodologically, and what must be true of the red team for the safety number to carry weight?
  3. Untrusted monitoring uses a monitor as capable as the attacker, strictly better than trusted monitoring on competence. Name its distinctive failure mode and the paper's countermeasure.
  4. Define both edges of the useful-but-catchable window, and state which quantity's growth closes it. Which legal instrument currently requires a control evaluation before a frontier lab deploys a model internally as an R&D assistant?

Answers. (1) Alignment tries to make the model not intend subversion, a model property; control tries to make intended subversion fail, a protocol property built from monitoring, restricted authority, and audits. Clean alignment evals are evidence about intent under test conditions, and W42 showed models can behave differently when they infer they are being tested; they bound the probability of scheming without touching the question control answers, which is what happens if that bound is wrong. The failures differ too: an alignment failure is silent until it matters, while a control failure requires beating a protocol built to make beating it expensive. (2) It converts an unmeasurable question, will this model attack, into a measurable one, can any attack policy we construct beat the protocol, yielding a worst-case-flavored bound that does not depend on trusting the model's disposition. It carries weight only under the elicitation assumption: the red team must find attacks at least as good as the deployed model could mount. (3) Collusion, since monitor and attacker are instances of the same untrusted model and an attack can carry a signal telling the monitor to wave it through. The countermeasure is upfront validation on inputs known to contain backdoors; a monitor that passes known backdoors convicts itself, and the protocol treats its scores accordingly. (4) Lower edge: transformatively useful, capable of substantially reducing the risk posed by subsequent AIs, chiefly by accelerating safety-relevant R&D. Upper edge: uncontrollable, capable enough at evading control techniques or sabotaging control evaluations that control is infeasible. The window closes as the capability gap between trusted labor and the untrusted model grows. No instrument requires a control evaluation: SB 53's transparency report attaches at deployment and its internal-use reach is a quarterly self-summary to Cal OES, while whether the EU AI Act reaches internal deployment at all is contested, with Article 55 attaching at market placement, Article 2(8) exempting what comes before, and Apollo arguing in-house use is already putting into service. In practice the artifact you just wrote answers to the lab's own published safety framework.
