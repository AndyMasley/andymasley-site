Tokenization and the language modeling objective

Read

  • Karpathy, "Let's build the GPT Tokenizer" (karpathy.ai/zero-to-hero) — at the keyboard: BPE from scratch, training a tokenizer, the GPT-2 and GPT-4 vocabularies, and the closing tour of model failures traceable to tokenization. Afterward implement the merge loop from memory, then check it against the video's code.
  • Stanford CS336, Lecture 1 "Overview, tokenization" + the BPE-tokenizer portion of Assignment 1 (Basics) (cs336.stanford.edu) — the lecture, then the assignment portion alongside the build. Change the vocabulary size in your Assignment 1 tokenizer and predict what happens to tokens-per-word before you rerun it.
  • Petrov et al., "Language Model Tokenizers Introduce Unfairness Between Languages" (arXiv:2305.15425) — the premium measurements: token counts for the same content across languages, and the consequences for cost, latency, and context window. Write a six-line evidence brief separating cost effects from performance claims.
  • Dive into Deep Learning (d2l.ai) — optional. 9.2 Converting Raw Text into Sequence Data, 9.3 Language Models (perplexity), 15.1 Word Embedding (word2vec), 4.1 Softmax Regression. Run its tokenization code on two languages and one technical corpus and keep the outputs.

Do

Code along with Karpathy's first makemore video: the count-based bigram, then the neural one (a single linear layer into softmax), and watch them land on the same loss. Before the first training step, predict the loss of uniform guessing (−log(1/27) for a 27-character vocabulary) and check how close the untrained network starts.

Then implement the BPE merge loop and train a small tokenizer on a corpus of your choosing. Retrain it at the same vocabulary size on a different-language corpus, predict which token counts move and in which direction, then measure.

Put the results in one notebook. Take the same short text (a parallel passage, not separate texts) in at least four languages spanning two scripts, run it through two tokenizers (a production GPT tokenizer via tiktoken, and your own), and tabulate tokens-per-sentence with each language's premium relative to English. Alongside it: both bigram models with their final losses, and three samples from each.

Done when the table shows tokens-per-sentence for the same content in 4+ languages, and directly beneath it one sentence states the API-cost implication using your own numbers ("at per-token prices, this request in ⟨language⟩ costs N× the English price"), where a reader can recompute N from the table alone.

Skip if you can already derive the autoregressive objective with every shape attached and say in two sentences why identical content is not identical cost across languages.

Take away

  • A language model is one probability distribution over token sequences, factored by the chain rule: p(x₁…x_T) = ∏ p(x_t | x_<t). Training minimizes the average negative log probability of the next token, which is W4's cross-entropy. Perplexity is that loss exponentiated.
  • A token is not a word, and it is not neutral. BPE is compression fit to a training corpus: strings the corpus saw often become single tokens, everything else gets shredded toward bytes.
  • Petrov et al. measure up to a 15× spread in tokenization length for the same translated content, and 4×+ gaps survive even byte-level encoding. APIs bill, rate-limit, and window by the token, so "per-token" pricing is per-language pricing.
  • The embedding matrix is V×d, one row per token; the output matrix is d×V, one column per token; softmax turns its logits into the distribution the loss scores. Sometimes the two are tied into one shared matrix, never interchangeable in role.
  • The same multiplier shrinks the effective context window. "128K context" is not one product across languages.
  • Any administration buying per-token API access for citizen-facing services (the EU institutions, operating in 24 official languages, are the sharpest case) is signing a contract with an undisclosed per-language price multiplier.
  • The tractable ask is disclosure, not redesign: publish tokens-per-character on a standard parallel corpus for every language in scope. The procurement framing is an application, not a documented case.
Check yourself
  1. Write the autoregressive factorization of p(x₁…x_T) and the training loss for one sequence. Which W4 quantity is the per-token loss, and what does perplexity mean in its terms?
  2. Your count-based bigram and your SGD-trained neural bigram converge to the same loss. What is the neural bigram, and why must the two agree?
  3. A BPE tokenizer trained mostly on English shreds Amharic into near-byte fragments. Name the mechanism, and the three consequences Petrov et al. draw at fixed per-token pricing and fixed context length.
  4. "The model handles spelling questions badly because it can't see letters." What is right in that sentence, and what does it get wrong about where the limitation lives?

Answers. (1) p(x₁…x_T) = ∏_t p(x_t | x_<t), an exact identity rather than an approximation; the loss is the average of −log p(x_t | x_<t), the cross-entropy between the one-hot next token and the predicted distribution, and perplexity is exp of that average, the model's uncertainty as an effective number of equally likely choices. (2) A softmax regression from a one-hot of the current token: one V×V weight matrix whose row i holds the logits for what follows token i. Counting computes the conditional MLE directly, gradient descent on cross-entropy converges to the same MLE, so they meet at the same loss; "neural" buys nothing until contexts get too numerous to count, which is what W8's MLP was for. (3) BPE merges are earned by corpus frequency, so scripts the corpus underrepresents never earn merges and their text stays near byte level, multiplying the token count. Consequences: higher API cost for identical content, higher latency (more sequential tokens to process and generate), and a smaller effective context window. (4) Right: the model receives token IDs and then embedding rows, never characters, so a spelling question asks about the internal structure of atoms it cannot decompose. Wrong: locating the limit in the model. It lives in the tokenizer, a replaceable pipeline choice, and character- or byte-level tokenization would expose the letters at the price of far longer sequences.
