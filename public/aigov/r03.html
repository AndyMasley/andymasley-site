R3: Federalism and state AI law

Read

  • Executive Order 14365 (White House, Dec 2025; text) — every asserted federal mechanism and the legal challenge it invites. You mapped this in R1; here, write the statutory hook each mechanism would need to work.
  • California Transparency in Frontier Artificial Intelligence Act, SB 53 (enacted text) — covered actors, duties, thresholds, reporting, enforcement, effective dates. Copy the thresholds down verbatim and list who sits just outside them.
  • Colorado Artificial Intelligence Act, SB 24-205 (bill) — deployer and developer duties, high risk decisions, discrimination, notice, enforcement, effective dates. Note every date that has moved and what moved it.

Do

The case. Put SB 1047 as passed beside SB 53 as enacted and derive the political constraint set from the diff alone, before you read the veto message. Dropped: the shutdown capability, third-party audits, the Board of Frontier Models, liability for derivative models. Kept: published frameworks, incident reporting, whistleblower protection, a compute-and-revenue gate. Give yourself an hour to write the half-page memo naming which cut bought which vote. Then read Newsom's veto message of 29 September 2024 and count what you attributed to the wrong opponent.

Compare one frontier model rule and one automated decision rule across authority, object, trigger, evidence, remedies, compliance cost, and interstate effects.

Then write a federalism options memorandum with model statutory language and a preemption analysis: which level of government holds the constitutional power, which holds statutory authority today, which has the institutional competence to run the program, and where those three come apart.

Done when the recommendation separates constitutional power, statutory authority, institutional competence, and political preference, and a drafter could lift your model language into a bill without rewriting it.

Take away

  • The two state laws regulate different objects. SB 53 reaches large frontier developers by capability and size: models trained above 10^26 operations, developers above $500M annual revenue.
  • SB 53's duties are published safety frameworks, transparency reports, critical safety incident reporting to Cal OES within 15 days, whistleblower protection, and AG enforcement up to $1M per violation.
  • The Colorado act reaches developers and deployers of systems making consequential decisions, and imposes a duty of reasonable care against algorithmic discrimination.
  • Enforcement design decides who complies. Both statutes route through the state attorney general with no private right of action, which caps exposure and makes compliance a function of one office's priorities and budget.
  • Colorado also offers an affirmative defense for programs aligned to the NIST AI RMF, which quietly converts a voluntary framework into a liability shield.
  • Preemption is a statutory question, and there is no AI statute to preempt with. The Senate stripped the ten-year state moratorium from the 2025 reconciliation bill 99-1. EO 14365 therefore works through proxies: DOJ litigation, funding conditions, and agency proceedings that each need their own hook.
  • Funding conditions have limits worth knowing before you propose one. Under Dole they must be unambiguous and related to the federal program's purpose, and NFIB bars conditions coercive enough to be compulsion. Conditioning BEAD broadband money on a state's AI law is where both tests get argued.
  • Dormant Commerce Clause attacks on state AI law are weaker than industry briefs suggest. After National Pork Producers v. Ross (2023), a nondiscriminatory law with incidental out-of-state burden usually survives.
  • Compliance dates are the live fight, not the duties. Colorado's obligations slipped to June 30, 2026 and the statute has faced repeal-and-replace pressure every session since enactment. Treat any effective date as a lobbying target.
Check yourself
  1. Why can't an executive order cure Congress's failure to pass a preemptive AI statute?
  2. Name three ways the objects of SB 53 and the Colorado AI Act differ.
  3. What are the constitutional limits on conditioning federal grant money on a state repealing its AI law?
  4. A developer argues a state AI law unconstitutionally burdens interstate commerce. How strong is that after National Pork Producers?

Answers. (1) Preemption flows from a federal statute conflicting with state law; an EO creates none, so it can only fund litigation, attach conditions to money Congress already appropriated, or push agencies to act under their own hooks. (2) Covered actor (large frontier developers by compute and revenue vs. developers and deployers of consequential decision systems); trigger (training scale vs. use in a specific decision about a person); duty (disclosure, framework publication, and incident reporting vs. reasonable care against algorithmic discrimination). (3) Dole: unambiguous, related to the funded program, within the spending power; NFIB: not coercive. Both are argued on the size and relatedness of the grant. (4) Weak on its own. Incidental burden from an evenhanded law is generally tolerated, and the extraterritoriality theory narrowed after Ross; the stronger challenges are statutory or First Amendment.
