C2: Red team and revise

Read

  • Klein, "Performing a Project Premortem" (HBR, September 2007) — all of it; it is two pages. Run the premortem as written, with stipulated failure and silent listing, before you reread your own draft.
  • The Alignment Faking external reviews (reviews PDF) — the W42 document, reread for form rather than content. Write one sentence per reviewer on what made the critique land.
  • ICD 203, Analytic Standards (ODNI, revised January 2015; dni.gov PDF, FAS mirror) — optional. The nine analytic tradecraft standards, especially the seventh. Read it as the rubric your revision log gets graded against.

Do

Every round writes rows into one red-team record: the attack in the attacker's voice, an axis tag, your written response, and a ruling of survived / revised (name the section) / conceded–unresolved.

Two axes. Technical: is the mechanism and evidence right? Institutional: is it implementable, given authority, venue, cost, enforcement? A finished record must carry both; a draft reviewed on one is half-reviewed.

Round 0, day one. Hand the draft to one named human and ask for one axis honestly covered; an IFP colleague reading for institutional realism is the intended case, and the tutor covers the other axis. Ask two specific questions: which section would you cut, and which claim would you bet against? Log name and date. Send on day one; their turnaround is the critical path.

Round 1, the three personas (TUTOR.md rule 10), at full strength with incentives attached. Write a response to every attack before ruling on it.

  • Frontier-lab GC: scope creep, safe-harbor gaps, liability exposure, the threshold that captures their safe system or waves a rival's through.
  • Accelerationist Hill staffer: cost, competitiveness, whose district pays, which committee has jurisdiction, why this instrument dies in markup.
  • Chinese MOFA analyst: reciprocity, verification asymmetry, what it signals, how a ministry white paper quotes your threshold back at you.

Rounds 2 and 3, the premortem and consider-the-opposite (rule 9). It is 2029, your recommendation was adopted and failed. Write the postmortem, naming the failure concretely: which world-model parameter broke, which actor defected, which measurement was gamed, which discretionary escape hatch got used.

Then take each load-bearing row of C1's claim ledger and ask whether you would have weighted this evidence the same had it cut the other way. The postmortem and every row that fails the symmetry check enter the record as attacks, with axis tags and rulings.

Round 4, revise, with the log. Every difference between the C1 draft and the revised draft enters the revision log tagged correction / judgment change / style, with a one-line reason and a pointer to the attack that forced it; style needs no attack.

Preserve verbatim what you could not answer; the strongest unresolved criticism travels to C3's defense. Ship one bundle: the revised draft, the record, and the log, with the human reader's name, date, and what their read changed.

Done when at least one attack produced a substantive revision traceable to a named attack; the log's tags separate corrections from judgment changes under spot-check, the tutor rerunning the sources behind two logged changes, either tag, with a correction hiding under a judgment tag failing the bundle; and a named human read the draft and left a trace in the record.

A record where every ruling reads "survived" fails, and so does a log that is all style. There is no pass task; the object under review is your own C1 draft.

Take away

  • Three kinds of change, three tags. A correction means your evidence was wrong, in a number, citation, authority, or mechanism, and anyone rerunning your sources converges on the fix.
  • A judgment change means the evidence stands but your weights moved, which is the analysis working rather than failing. Style is neither, tagged so it cannot pad the count.
  • The split matters downstream: in C3 and the annual what-I-got-wrong post, corrections are confessed and judgment changes are argued.
  • Surviving unchanged is not strength. A record where every attack reads "survived" means the attacks ran below full strength or the rulings weren't honest. Conceding everything is the symmetric failure, a draft whose claims were never priced.
  • Klein's result explains why stipulated failure works: people under-generate failure reasons for plans they own.
  • The premortem stipulates adopted-and-failed. A rejection postmortem collapses into politics and tests only your persuasion; adopted-and-failed holds the politics fixed and forces the recommendation's own failure mechanism into view.
  • ICD 203's seventh tradecraft standard, consistency with or change from previous analysis, is the revision log's standard applied to yourself.
  • Each persona is also a real downstream document: the GC's comment letter, the staffer's one-pager that kills your instrument in markup, the ministry statement quoting your threshold back. Running that response now costs an evening instead of a public walk-back.
  • The named human is the one part of the review no AI can flatter. DEPTH's counting rule is blunt: a quarter with no named-human exchange didn't count.
Check yourself
  1. Tag these three changes, and say why the distinction survives into C3: (a) your draft stated an enacted threshold one order of magnitude high against the statute's text; (b) after the MOFA round you weight verification asymmetry more heavily and downgrade "adopt" to "pilot"; (c) you moved the recommendation above the evidence summary because your reader stopped early.
  2. Why must the premortem stipulate adopted and failed rather than rejected?
  3. Match the attack to the persona structurally positioned to make it: (a) your reporting threshold also captures a class of systems you never meant to reach; (b) your recommendation requires an appropriation nobody will move; (c) your verification ask is non-reciprocal and reads as containment.
  4. The record shows twelve attacks and twelve "survived" rulings, and your human reader returned "looks right to me" with no notes. What does the acceptance test do with the bundle?

Answers. (1) (a) Correction: anyone rerunning the source converges on the fix. (b) Judgment change: the evidence stands, the weights moved. (c) Style: no attack required, tagged so it can't pad the count. The distinction survives to C3 and the annual what-I-got-wrong post, where corrections are confessed and judgment changes are argued. (2) Rejection tests persuasion, and its postmortem collapses into politics: wrong venue, wrong framing, wrong week. Adopted-and-failed holds the politics fixed and exposes the recommendation's own failure mechanism: broken parameter, defecting actor, gamed measurement, used escape hatch. (3) (a) The frontier-lab GC: compliance teams read definitions for scope, not intent. (b) The Hill staffer, whose terrain is appropriations and political economy. (c) The MOFA analyst, since reciprocity and signaling are what a ministry reads first. (4) It fails twice. All-survived means the personas ran below full strength or the rulings were advocacy dressed as refereeing; rerun the weakest round at full strength rather than invent a concession. "Looks right" leaves no trace in the record, so the named-human condition isn't met either.
