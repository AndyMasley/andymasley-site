R5: Copyright, training data, identity, and open weights

Read

  • Copyright and Artificial Intelligence reports (US Copyright Office, hub) — the three reports on digital replicas, copyrightability, and training, plus the cited authorities. Mark where each states existing law and where it recommends legislation.
  • C2PA Technical Specification 2.2 (spec) — claims, manifests, signing, trust, ingredients, redaction, threat model, limitations. Read the threat model first, then list what a valid manifest does not prove.
  • Model Openness Framework (Linux Foundation AI and Data, isitopen.ai) — source, weights, data, documentation, licensing, reproducibility, downstream freedoms. Score two releases that both call themselves open.

Do

The case. It is February 2019. Your lab has trained a language model in four sizes, the largest at 1.5 billion parameters, and the release decision is yours: all weights now, staged by size, or hold. Nothing has shipped, so there is no misuse record to reason from. Give yourself an hour for the half-page recommendation, saying which of the four openness variables each option moves and what evidence would reverse you. Then read Solaiman et al., Release Strategies and the Social Impacts of Language Models (arXiv:1908.09203), and NTIA's 2024 report on open model weights for what a government made of the same question five years later.

Analyze one proposed training or release practice from the creator, developer, deployer, researcher, and affected person perspectives. Produce an options memorandum with licensing, provenance, transparency, access, liability, and enforcement provisions, and name for each option the empirical question whose answer would change your recommendation.

Done when the product avoids treating all openness or all data rights as one variable and identifies the empirical crux behind each tradeoff.

Skip if you can already say which of the four openness variables a given release moves.

Take away

  • The Copyright Office split the problem three ways: digital replicas (Part 1, July 2024), copyrightability of AI output (Part 2, January 2025), and training as fair use (Part 3, pre-publication May 2025). The split is useful even where you disagree with the conclusions.
  • Registration requires human authorship. Prompting alone does not supply it; human selection, arrangement, or modification can.
  • Fair use for training is decided on a record, not on a technology. Bartz v. Anthropic and Kadrey v. Meta (N.D. Cal., June 2025) both treated training as transformative on those records, leaving acquisition method and market dilution as the live questions.
  • A C2PA manifest proves who signed, not that the content is true. It binds assertions to a signer and a hash. Metadata strips silently, absence of a manifest means nothing, and a compromised or lying signer produces a valid claim.
  • Openness is at least four independent variables: code, weights, training data, documentation, each with its own license and downstream freedoms. A weights release is the one decision in this unit that cannot be reversed.
  • Each tradeoff has a matching instrument: contract and licensing for data, statute for digital replicas (state ELVIS-type laws, a federal NO FAKES-style bill), procurement and disclosure rules for provenance, liability allocation for downstream deployment.
Check yourself
  1. Your client's model output is commercially valuable. What has to be true for any of it to be registrable?
  2. A platform mandates C2PA manifests on every upload. Which harms does that leave untouched?
  3. Two labs both announce "open source" models. Which distinctions does that phrase hide?
  4. Which risks of a training practice can licensing fix, and which survive a signed license?

Answers. (1) Human authorship the Office recognizes: selection and arrangement of AI-generated material, or human-authored elements within the work. The prompt does not count, and the registration must disclaim machine-generated portions. (2) Anything about truth or consent. It leaves authentic-but-misleading capture, defamatory real footage, unlicensed training on signed content, and unsigned content whose missing manifest proves nothing. (3) Whether weights, code, training data, and documentation are each released, under what license, with what use restrictions, and whether a third party can reproduce the result. (4) Licensing can authorize a corpus, allocate indemnity, and set field-of-use limits. It cannot fix third-party rights the licensor never held, publicity and digital-replica claims from people depicted in the data, or downstream liability for outputs.
