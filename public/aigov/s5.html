Evals-gated governance

Read

  • Anthropic RSP v3.4 and the version archive (responsible-scaling-policy · rsp-updates) — the text for exercise 1, the corpus for exercise 2. Read it the way opposing counsel would: who could I hold to this, and how would I know if they broke it?
  • Karnofsky, "If-Then Commitments for AI Risk Reduction" (carnegieendowment.org) — the CBRN worked example, the tripwire-operationalization section, and the limitations he concedes. Then ask whether v3.4 allocates trigger-writing and threshold-moving power the way its logic demands.
  • Clymer et al., "Safety Cases: How to Justify the Safety of Advanced AI Systems" (arXiv:2403.10462) — the four argument categories (inability, control, trustworthiness, deference) and how a full case composes them; skip the worked-example detail. For each ASL gate in your red-lined RSP, name which type it instantiates.
  • OpenAI Preparedness Framework v2 (openai.com · PDF) — optional comparator: three tracked categories, two thresholds, with regulatory mapping split into the May 2026 Frontier Governance Framework. Diff its threshold language against the RSP you red-lined.
  • SaferAI risk-management ratings (tracker.safer-ai.org) — optional. Twelve companies scored 8–35% across risk identification, analysis, treatment, and governance. Compare their Risk Analysis score for the company you red-lined against your own hatch count.

Do

Exercise 1, the red-line. Read RSP v3.4 end to end, marking every discretionary escape hatch: modal verbs ("may", "we expect", "as appropriate"), undefined thresholds, self-adjudicated tests ("if we determine"), obligations with no named owner or date. Write your classification rule first, then classify each clause hard commitment or discretionary hatch.

Exercise 2, the version diff. All nine versions, v1.0 (19 September 2023) through v3.4, are archived at rsp-updates. Official redlines exist for v2.2 and v3.1–v3.4 only, so hand-diff the two structural rewrites, 1.0→2.0 and 2.2→3.0. Track thresholds by construct (capability, safeguard, who decides) rather than by section number.

Produce two documents: the red-lined RSP, an annotated excerpt list headed by your classification rule and the tally N discretionary hatches, M hard commitments; and the version-diff memo, one page, on the ratchet-down hypothesis. R3 has the SB 53 statutory read.

Then spend twenty minutes on the matrix, writing into matrix/matrix.md in the repo: cell-ify the evals-sufficiency debate for at least the safety-tax-collapse and scheming rows (sign, conditional, crux) and date your credence in cruxes.md.

Done when the tally line states N and M with every counted item quoted under the stated rule; the memo cites at least three textual changes by version number, assigns each a direction, renders an explicit verdict on the ratchet hypothesis (a "mixed" verdict passes only if it says which constructs moved which way), and connects the SB 53 disclosure-wins lesson to the diffability of the archive it relied on.

Skip if you have already annotated a frontier safety framework clause by clause with discretionary language flagged and counted, and can state which way its thresholds moved across at least two versions.

Take away

  • A hard commitment has three parts: a named actor, a checkable trigger, and a dated or otherwise verifiable duty. Anything missing one is a hatch. SaferAI's version of the point: an RSP without a defined risk tolerance is risk management in name only.
  • The architecture is a ladder, RSP → safety case → audit → licensing, and each rung moves threshold-moving power further from the developer. A self-written policy is checked by its author; licensing is the state grading before deployment. No rung above the first binds in US law today.
  • Discretion is not disproof, and revision is not retreat. A framework with zero "may" would pretend W36–W40's instruments can bear rule-grade precision. The ratchet-down hypothesis (Khlaaf & Myers West, arXiv:2504.15088) is an empirical claim about direction, readable only in the text.
  • Karnofsky's argument is that if-then commitments cost near-nothing if the trigger capabilities never arrive and bind if they do, so short- and long-timeline camps can sign the same text. Take it seriously and the fight relocates onto who writes the triggers, who runs the evals, and who can move the thresholds.
  • Every disclosure instrument now in force (SB 53, the EU AI Act's GPAI obligations) compels publication of frameworks like the one you red-lined, and none validates the measurement or accredits an auditor. California's substantive-mandate bill died by veto and its disclosure bill became law, so disclosure is the regime we run.
Check yourself
  1. Which version-to-version steps in the 2023→2026 RSP history have official redlines, which don't, and why does that distribution put the hand-diff work where the ratchet hypothesis is hardest to test?
  2. Reconstruct Karnofsky's worldview-robustness argument in two sentences, and state what it predicts about where the political fight moves once everyone has signed an if-then regime.
  3. Name Clymer et al.'s four safety-case argument types. Which do current ASL-style capability gates mostly instantiate, and which does W42's evaluation-awareness evidence stress?
  4. Give one piece of text-history evidence that complicates a simple ratchet-down story, and say what SB 53 changes about the ratchet dynamic and what it cannot change.

Answers. (1) Official redlines: v2.2 and v3.1 through v3.4. None for 1.0→2.0, 2.0→2.1, and 2.2→3.0. The two unredlined structural rewrites are where section structure changed and thresholds were re-founded rather than edited, so direction can't be read off a diff tool and each construct must be tracked by hand, which is where a ratchet would hide. (2) If-then commitments cost nearly nothing if trigger capabilities never arrive and bind if they do, so signing requires no agreement on timelines. Commitment-signing is therefore the cheap phase, and the fight relocates to trigger-writing, eval-running, and threshold-moving: the powers your red-line audited. (3) Inability, control, trustworthiness, deference. ASL-style gates are mostly inability arguments: the model cannot yet do X, as measured pre-deployment. Evaluation awareness stresses inability at its measurement joint, since a test-aware model can sandbag the eval the gate is written on, and spills into trustworthiness as behavioral evidence stops discriminating safe from test-savvy. (4) Any of: v3.2 giving the Long-Term Benefit Trust approval power over the selection of external reviewers; v3.1's clarification that pausing development remains available regardless of RSP requirements; v3.3's novel-CBRN threshold rewritten to track the threat model more closely. SB 53 makes the framework and its revisions legally disclosable, so private revision becomes public, diffable text and the ratchet is at least observable. It cannot stop a threshold from moving, validate the measurement behind it, or put anyone outside the company in the approval loop.
