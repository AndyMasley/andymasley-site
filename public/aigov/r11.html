R11: Energy, infrastructure, environment, and communities

Read

  • Docket RM26-4, Interconnection of Large Loads to the Interstate Transmission System (FERC, docket page) — notices, orders, comments, jurisdictional positions, thresholds, cost allocation. Read one utility comment and one consumer advocate comment on the same question.
  • Data center and electricity demand data (EIA, Form EIA-860M) — capacity, generator, load, and regional data with the definitions and revision notes. Work out what the form can and cannot tell you about load.
  • eGRID (EPA, program page) — the methods first, then subregional emissions, generation, and resource mix. Write the sentence you would use to describe a facility's emissions honestly with only this dataset.
  • Environmental Justice Index (CDC/ATSDR, EJI) — optional; the cumulative-burden screen. EPA's EJScreen was removed 5 February 2025 and survives only through the Public Environmental Data Partners mirror (screening-tools.com).

Do

The case. PUCO case 24-508-EL-ATA. AEP Ohio has proposed a tariff for new data-center load above 25 MW, the evidentiary hearing ran from December 2024 into January 2025, and a settlement signed by some parties and not others sits with the commission. You staff a commissioner. One hour, half a page: approve, modify, or reject, and say which costs the minimum-take percentage and the contract term allocate, who carries the risk if the load arrives late or never arrives, and what you would want on the record before the next case. Then check against the July 2025 order.

Evaluate one proposed large load, a real project in a live interconnection queue. Keep four layers separate and labeled: measured facts, engineering estimates, forecasts, policy judgments.

Produce the diligence model (energy, power, capacity, utilization, timing); the authority map covering FERC, the RTO or ISO, the state commission, the siting board, county zoning, and air and water permits, with every appeal path and deadline; a ratepayer and reliability briefing written for a commissioner; a lifecycle impact assessment using subregional data; and a community participation plan built on real comment dates.

Done when the product distinguishes energy from power and capacity, allocates costs to a cause, tests timing and utilization sensitivities, uses geographic rather than national-average evidence, and identifies every appeal path.

Skip if you have already built this for a real project. Then do only the authority map with appeal paths and filed comment deadlines.

Take away

  • Energy, power, and capacity answer different questions. A 500 MW campus is a power rating; at 80% utilization it consumes roughly 3.5 TWh a year; capacity is what the system must hold available at peak. Tariff design needs the third, emissions math the second, headlines use the first.
  • Jurisdiction is the live question. FERC governs transmission service and wholesale sales under Federal Power Act sections 205 and 206. Retail service, generation siting, and distribution stay with the states under section 201(b). Large-load interconnection has been retail utility business, which is what RM26-4 unsettles.
  • Cost allocation follows cost causation: rates must be roughly commensurate with the burdens a customer imposes. The working instruments are a large-load tariff class, minimum-take and contract-term commitments, collateral, and exit fees, all for the case where the load arrives late, leaves early, or never shows.
  • Queue totals are an upper bound, not a forecast. The same project shops several utilities and holds speculative positions in more than one queue, so summing announced megawatts double-counts by construction.
  • EIA-860M inventories generators with planned online dates, so data-center demand has to be inferred from other evidence. eGRID publishes annual subregional averages with a long lag, and a new round-the-clock load is served by marginal units, so an average understates the emissions caused.
  • Policy relevance: NEPA attaches only where there is a federal handle, meaning federal land, a federal permit, or federal money, and Seven County Infrastructure Coalition v. Eagle County (2025) narrowed how far upstream and downstream an agency must look and told courts to defer on scoping.
  • The leverage left is state and local: commission tariff proceedings, air permits for backup generation and onsite turbines, water permits, and county zoning, each with its own clock.
Check yourself
  1. A developer announces a 1 GW campus. What else do you need to state its annual energy use and its capacity obligation?
  2. Which parts of the approval chain does FERC not control, and where does each order get appealed?
  3. Why is an eGRID subregional average the wrong number for "the emissions this data center causes"?
  4. Two utilities each report a 2 GW request from the same developer. How much demand is that?

Answers. (1) For energy: the load factor and the ramp schedule, since 1 GW at 80% is about 7 TWh a year and a phased build is much less in year one. For capacity: whether the load is coincident with system peak, whether it is firm or curtailable, and the reserve margin the system carries. (2) Retail rates, generation and facility siting, distribution service, land use, and most environmental permitting are state or local. A state commission order goes to rehearing and then to state court; a FERC order requires a rehearing request within 30 days before review in a federal court of appeals under FPA section 313. (3) It is an annual average across all generation in the subregion, while a new constant load is served at the margin by whatever unit dispatches next, and the average also ignores capacity built to serve it. Use a dispatch model or a marginal-emissions tool such as AVERT. (4) Somewhere between zero and 2 GW. Queue positions are options rather than commitments, and shopping one project across utilities inflates every regional total that adds them up.
