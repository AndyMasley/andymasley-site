R1: US AI policy architecture

Read

For each instrument, record: issuer, authority, legal status, required action, deadline, implementing institution, litigation exposure, successor. That table is the residency's standing habit.

  • America's AI Action Plan (White House, July 2025; PDF) — the three pillars and every action assigned to an institution. Mark which of its ~90 actions carry authority and which are aspiration on letterhead.
  • Executive Order 14365 (Dec 2025; text) — the litigation task force, funding conditions, preemption theory, deadlines, legal limits. Record each asserted mechanism and its limit.
  • NSPM-11 (June 2026; text) — the adoption, assurance, accountability, compute, security and reporting directives, with owners and deadlines.

Do

The case. Read the EO 14110 record up to 20 January 2025 only: issued Oct 2023, implemented through OMB M-24-10 and DPA §4.2 reporting. Give yourself an hour to write the half-page memo an incoming NSC staffer needed: what does rescission actually delete, and what survives it? Then check against what happened.

The memo. Two pages, BLUF, to a named principal (an IFP colleague heading to a Hill briefing, or a state legislative staffer): the operative map of US AI authority this month. What binds, what is litigation-exposed (DOJ task force, X.AI v. Weiser, Colorado's repeal-and-replace), what is vacuum. Recommendation first, three options, one page of map.

Done when every consequential claim links to controlling text, legal force is distinguished from political announcement throughout, and a reader can act from page one. Then compare against a real CRS report on the same ground and count the levers you missed.

Take away

  • Five instrument types carry almost everything: statute, executive order, agency rule, guidance, funding condition. Knowing which one you're holding is the skill this unit exists for.
  • An EO cannot preempt state law. Preemption needs a statute to conflict with, so EO 14365 works through proxies instead: DOJ litigation, funding conditions on BEAD, and agency preemption strategies that each need their own hook.
  • EO provisions anchored in statute survive; free-floating ones are press releases with deadlines. EO 14110's DPA §4.2 reporting had real authority behind it, and its compute thresholds outlived the EO by migrating into state law.
  • Classified annexes, directive verbs, no notice-and-comment: that is the national-security instrument type, and NSPM-11 is the current example.
  • Strategy documents are unreviewable because they do nothing. You challenge the agency action that follows, under the APA, not the plan.
  • Durability ranking: statute > agency rule (notice-and-comment cuts both ways) > IEEPA-anchored EO section > OMB memo, which the next director can rescind before lunch.
Check yourself
  1. Why can't EO 14365 preempt state law itself, and what three proxies does it use?
  2. Which provision of the rescinded EO 14110 had the strongest statutory anchor, and why does that matter?
  3. The Action Plan "directs" an agency to do something no statute authorizes. What happens, and who could challenge it?
  4. Rank by durability: OMB memo, BIS final rule, IEEPA-anchored EO section, signed statute.

Answers. (1) Preemption flows from statute; an EO creates none. Proxies: DOJ litigation on existing-law and dormant-commerce theories, discretionary funding conditions, and FTC/FCC preemption strategies that need their own statutory hooks. (2) DPA §4.2 training-run reporting, which borrowed the Defense Production Act's authority. (3) Nothing self-executing; an agency must find authority elsewhere or proceed as guidance, and a regulated party challenges the resulting action, not the plan. (4) Statute > BIS rule > EO section > OMB memo.
