Massachusetts, unrolled

Click anywhere to go there. The top edge meets the bottom edge. The dark part is the hull's glass.

What is real here

The whole Commonwealth is laid on the inside of a spinning cylinder at one to one. The map frame is Massachusetts State Plane metres (the same grid the Webster drive uses), so a metre driven here is a metre there. East to west runs down the axis. North to south wraps around, which is why the land climbs the walls and the far side hangs overhead.

Length329.9 km, Berkshires end to Atlantic end

Windows37% of the hull is glass, where New York, Vermont, New Hampshire, Connecticut and Rhode Island would be (O'Neill's Island Three alternated strips of land and window). Through it the sky turns once every 5 min 47 s. The habitat is drawn at L5, so the Earth is 1.9° across and the Moon half a degree, sixty degrees apart.

AirThins toward the axis as spin gravity fades. The blue overhead is real scattering through about 24 km of sea-level-equivalent air.
SunA lamp that rides the axis from the Atlantic cap at 6 to the Berkshire cap at 18, so it is noon under it and evening far from it, all at once.

Measured

ElevationNear you the ground is the bare earth of the LiDAR surveys (the ones the buildings and trees come from, below), read onto nodes 8 m apart. Farther off it is USGS 3DEP 1 arc-second elevation (about 30 m) on a 46 m grid (92 m on phones). No vertical exaggeration. On the LiDAR ground Greylock's summit comes out at 1,064.4 m against a surveyed 1,063.4 m, and Mount Tom at 365.5 m against 366.4 m.
StreetsEvery public road in OpenStreetMap: about 68,000 km and 57,000 names, with one-way carriageways and lane counts where they are tagged. Each street's paved width (travel lanes and shoulders), lane count and sidewalks come from MassDOT's road inventory where it lies along an inventory segment (about 150,000 ways; the rest keep a width by class and lane count). A street's height is the LiDAR ground under it, read every 4 m along its length. HOV-only lanes and ramps closed to cars (the Southeast Expressway's zipper lane among them) are left out, and where the two carriageways of a divided road lie closer together in the map than their lanes are wide, both are narrowed until they just meet.
BridgesAbout 8,500 stretches of road and track that OpenStreetMap tags as bridges (7,250 on streets, 1,290 on railways) stand at the height the LiDAR measured for their decks, which it did for 95% of their length. The Sagamore Bridge crosses the canal 46 m up and the Bourne Bridge 45 m up, the Braga Bridge is at 47 m, and the Tobin's upper deck runs from 45 to 53 m. An overpass passes over the road beneath it. The Federal Highway Administration's National Bridge Inventory (2025) is matched to 4,640 of its 5,311 Massachusetts structures: each matched bridge takes its design and material (slab, girder, truss, arch, suspension, cable-stayed, movable), and a girder bridge's beams are as deep as its longest span calls for.
Rails, runways, riversOpenStreetMap again: about 3,300 km of railway track on the surface (sidings and yards included), 114 km of runways and 214 km of taxiways at their tagged widths where they have one, and the centre lines of about 1,470 km of river too narrow to appear in the water map. Where NOAA's 1 m land cover (2016) shows water across one of those rivers, it is as wide as that water: 664 km of them, most between 6 and 31 m wide.
StreamsOpenStreetMap's streams, brooks and small canals (November 2026): about 12,700 km of them, each in a small channel with water in it. A stream runs at the lowest of the LiDAR ground along its line and 4 and 8 m to either side of it (never more than 2 m under the ground at the line), and it never climbs more than 2 m along its course, so it cuts through a hump such as a road's embankment over an unmapped culvert. It is as wide as its tag says where it has one (90 do). Ditches and drains are left out, and so are the stretches that run in culverts (a brook stops at the road it passes under), across the water map's ponds and rivers, or under buildings.
Buildings2.67 million OpenStreetMap footprints (mostly from MassGIS), less 10,210 that the LiDAR shows are not there (it saw only bare ground inside the outline, and no assessor's record has a building there put up since the survey), and 80,255 that the map lacks, traced from the LiDAR's building returns where they stand more than 2 m from every mapped outline. A piece narrower than 2 m, or a small one standing more than three times as tall as it is wide, is taken for a wall, the edge of a tower whose outline is drawn off it, a crane or a tree, and left out. About 2.56 million buildings stand on their real outlines with measured roofs: rectangular sections in the building's own frame, each under a few planes fitted to the LiDAR's roof returns, which is how the Webster drive measures its roofs. Where the survey filed too few returns as building, as it does for most houses under trees, the roof is fitted to the lowest last return in each half-metre cell, the one that came down through the leaves (about 650,000 roofs). A wing set at an angle to the rest is fitted in its own frame (17,421 buildings). About 29,000 buildings whose new fit did not hold up keep the fit from the earlier, sparser pass. Wall heights and storey counts follow the measured eaves. The 2,234 courtyards inside 1,612 of them, mapped in OpenStreetMap as holes in their outlines, are open to the sky, with their own walls and windows. The rest (mostly sheds and small garages with too few returns, and buildings newer than the survey) are still rectangles fitted to their outlines, about 130,000 of them at the height the LiDAR measured where it saw them but could not fit a roof. About 2,500 fits did not hold up and were thrown out, so those buildings are rectangles too. Nearly all were small buildings under tall trees, which the fit had turned into thin towers. 110 came out 45 m tall or more outside the cities with no tagged height to back that up, and seven sat under what was probably low cloud.
Over the roadWhere OpenStreetMap says a street or a railway runs under a building (a covered way, or a building passage), the building is open over it: 69 buildings, among them the Star Market in Newtonville, which spans the Massachusetts Turnpike, and TD Garden over North Station's tracks. The opening is as wide as the road and a metre to either side (3 m for a railway, its platforms included) and as high as the road needs: 5.5 m over a highway or a railway, 4.2 to 4.9 m over a street. The rest of the building stands on the ground, and you can drive through. Another 84 buildings OpenStreetMap raises off the ground (skybridges, canopies, upper storeys over a passage) are open underneath from their tagged height, or from 3.5 m under their tops.
RadioThe FCC's station lists (its FM and AM queries, November 2026, and its engineering database of January 2024 for directional antenna patterns, AM tower arrays and the station each translator relays): every FM station, low-power station, translator and booster and every AM station within 450 km of the middle of the state that puts a usable signal anywhere in it, 1,124 on FM and 354 on AM, and 29 big AM stations from farther off (WLW, WGN, KDKA, WSB and the like) that come in at night by skywave. Each one's field is worked out for every 294 m of the state on FM and every 1.5 km on AM from its licensed power, height and pattern over the USGS terrain: FM by the ITU's delta-Bullington diffraction method (P.526 and P.1812), AM by ground-wave theory over land and sea, by day and by night. So a station fades out behind the hills it really fades behind, two stations on one frequency trade places where they really meet, and at sunset the AM stations cut their power, turn their patterns or go off the air as their licenses say. 666 of them play live: the station's own stream, or for a translator or network station with none, the stream of the station or network it relays. They were found in radio-browser.info, iHeartRadio's directory and the streaming servers stations commonly use, and play straight from the station, so you hear whatever is on the air. The rest have no stream a web page can play and come in as a quiet carrier.
Assessor recordsMassGIS's standardized assessor parcels for every city and town but Boston, which keeps its own (the next row). The 1.78 million footprints that stand on a parcel with a record take its use, building style, storeys and year built: a single-family house the assessor calls a Cape is a Cape, a three-family of three storeys is a triple-decker, a mill is a mill, and a Dutch colonial gets its gambrel roof. A record describes a parcel's main building, so the smaller footprints on a house lot are its garages and sheds.
Boston and CambridgeThe cities' own assessing records (Analyze Boston's property assessment for 2026 and the Cambridge Property Database), joined to about 117,000 buildings through the cities' parcels: each building's type, storeys and year built, its roof shape (a mansard is drawn as a gambrel) and what its walls are (brick or stone, wood or vinyl siding, wood shingles, stucco, concrete, glass or metal).
Roof coloursThe MassGIS 2025 aerial (leaf-off, about 0.44 m a pixel as read here): each footprint's roof colour is the median of the pixels in the middle of its outline, leaving out leaves, shade and glare, mapped to roofing albedo the way the Webster drive maps it. Buildings taller than about 25 m lean in the photograph, so their roofs keep a colour drawn by rule, as do roofs the reading could not see.
TreesThe trees within about 30 m of a street or 40 m of a building are the ones the survey found, 15.8 million of them: the tops of its canopy height grid (2 m cells), each at its measured height. About 2,000 of the measured "trees" stood far taller than the woods around them and were left out (they were most likely masts, cranes, bridge towers and stray returns, and about 650 of them sat in what looks like a low cloud over Nantucket). Deeper in the woods the trees are placed to match the canopy cover and canopy height the LiDAR measured, on a 31 m grid.
LiDARUSGS 3DEP point clouds: the 2021 survey for central and eastern Massachusetts, and surveys from 2011 to 2015 for the west, the coast and the islands. A building or tree newer than its survey is estimated or missing, and a road built since runs over the ground as it was.
WaterOpenStreetMap lakes, ponds and river polygons (about 12,700 still waters) and its coastline. Surface levels are read from the USGS elevation, which is flattened over water.
LighthousesThe Coast Guard's Light List for the First District (MarineCadastre.gov's aids to navigation, November 2025): the 665 lit aids in the state's waters, 57 of them lighthouses, 159 lights on piles, beacons and small towers and 449 lit buoys, each at its listed height with its own colour, period and pattern of flashes. The 17 long-range flashing lights are drawn turning, so a flash is the beam sweeping past you, and where you stand sets when you see it. The 69 towers stand where the list or OpenStreetMap puts a lighthouse, dark ones too, at the height either gives and in the colours the list describes. Their shapes and the reach of the beams are drawn by rule.
FerriesOpenStreetMap's ferry routes (November 2026): 57 services in and around the state's waters, from the island boats out of Woods Hole and Hyannis and the fast boats to Provincetown and Nantucket to the harbour ferries in Boston and the Chappy ferry in Edgartown. The boat from New York leaves the map, so it is left out. Each runs on its mapped course and makes the crossing in its tagged time, docking included, in the island car ferry where the route carries cars and in a harbour launch where it does not. Where the 46 m water map cannot hold a course, the boat docks a little off its slip or takes the nearest way round through the water. When they sail is invented: a route's boats shuttle back and forth around the clock, evenly spaced.
Retaining wallsOpenStreetMap's retaining walls (November 2026): 2,336 of them, 149 km in all, from the walls along the Pike's cut through Newton to low walls along railways and gardens. Each stands where it is mapped. Its height is the step in the LiDAR ground across it (each side's ground, 8 to 12 m out, carried in to the wall's line), smoothed along the wall, or its tagged height where the ground shows no step; a wall holding back a road stands on the road. The ground steps at a wall instead of sloping through it, and the car stops at one. Cuts with no wall mapped along them still slope back, at about 45 degrees where they are deep.
Signs and signalsOpenStreetMap (November 2026): 11,530 traffic signals, 19,089 stop signs, 903 yield signs and 1,988 railway level crossings, each where it is mapped and facing the traffic the map says it is for (where it does not say, the traffic heading into the junction it stands just short of). The 65,477 crosswalks the map says are marked are painted, in the pattern it gives where it gives one (14,243 ladders, 9,270 zebras, 7,601 pairs of lines and 109 dashed), and a stop line is painted before each stop sign and signal. Crossings the map does not say are marked are left bare. Fire hydrants (9,403), bus stops (12,157, 718 of them with shelters), street lamps (12,682) and flagpoles (1,836, as tall as they are tagged where they are) stand where they are mapped.
Power linesOpenStreetMap again: 6,950 km of overhead line, 1,400 km of it at 345 kV, 3,170 km at 115 to 230 kV, 640 km at 69 kV and the rest lower or untagged, strung between its mapped towers and poles (about 70,000 of them).
GroundsOpenStreetMap again: 15,202 ball fields and courts (4,925 for tennis, 3,423 for baseball and softball, 2,337 for basketball, 1,685 for soccer, lacrosse, field hockey and rugby, and 399 for football among them), 476 running tracks, 10,064 outdoor pools, 3,310 playgrounds, 43,026 golf greens, fairways, tees, bunkers and stretches of rough, and 16,487 parking spaces, each painted where it is mapped. On a ball field, the bare ground the 1 m land cover saw (the infield) stays bare.
Masts and towersOpenStreetMap's masts, communication towers, chimneys, water towers and wind turbines, each as tall as the LiDAR measured it: the highest return within 6 m of its mapped point (within 15 m where nothing stood that close, and within 60 m of a turbine, whose blades sweep that far) over the ground under it, unless OpenStreetMap tags a height that does not agree, and then the tag (the thin top of a tall mast is easily missed). 1,948 in all: 1,589 masts and towers (1,570 of them measured; the tallest, in Needham, 391.5 m), 164 chimneys, 52 water towers and 143 wind turbines. Masts under 12 m, ones the LiDAR missed and the map gives no height, water towers a building already stands for and the turbines out at sea past the map's edge are left out.
Land coverWithin about 2 km of you, NOAA's high-resolution land cover at 1 m (C-CAP, from 2016 imagery): paving and roofs, lawns, crops, hay and pasture, grassland, broadleaf and evergreen woods, scrub, wooded and shrub swamp, marsh, beach, bare ground and small ponds, each where it was in 2016. Woods among lawns and paving are drawn as yard trees over grass. Farther off, NLCD 2025 land cover and tree canopy at 30 m: built-up land, canopy percentage, the evergreen share of the forest, cropland, pasture, marsh, sand and bare ground.
Town linesUS Census Bureau cartographic boundaries, 1:500,000, 2022 (all 351 cities and towns).
Dunkin'All the Places (alltheplaces.xyz), its copy of Dunkin's own store locator, October 31, 2026: 1,025 shops in Massachusetts.
WebsterGround heights inside the town come from the Webster drive's own LiDAR terrain (MassGIS). Its streets, buildings and trees are drawn the same way as every other town's.

Authored

Where a building is not measured, its height is estimated: about 26,000 footprints carry a tagged height or storey count and eleven named towers carry their surveyed heights, and the rest are sized from the footprint, the kind of building and how built-up the surroundings are. Where there is no assessor's record (a footprint on no parcel, or one the state's or the city's records do not reach), the kind of building (house, cape, ranch, triple-decker, rowhouse, shop, mill and so on) is guessed from the footprint's size and shape, its OpenStreetMap tag where it has one, and its neighbours. The front door goes on the side nearest a street. Outside Boston and Cambridge a wall's material is drawn by rule from the kind of building, its age and where it stands (wood shingles are commonest on the Cape and the Islands). Siding, trim, door and shutter colours are drawn by chance from the Webster drive's reads of 4,775 assessor photographs of its houses, so the mix of colours is Webster's and no one house's is its own. Windows and doors are placed by rule, in the manner of the Webster drive and with its surface textures, and so are roof shapes, porches and chimneys where the roof is not measured. A measured roof is simplified: anything smaller than about a metre is left out, except its chimneys, which stand where the LiDAR saw them, to their measured tops (1.18 million of them; on anything but a house they are drawn as vents and plant rooms). Its dormers are the ones the fit found, mostly shed dormers along a cape's or a bungalow's slope. Where the fit has a roof rising more steeply than a roof can (it happens on glass towers, where returns come off the walls and through the glass), that part is drawn flat. Away from you a building is drawn as plain boxes, one for each section of its measured roof, and the smaller the building the sooner it drops out of sight (a shed at a few hundred metres, a house further). The 35,000 big ones (15 m tall or more, or with 4,000 m² of roof) stay in sight for about a kilometre per metre of their height, up to 65 km, past the first few kilometres as just their tallest and widest boxes. Where the LiDAR saw no canopy to go by, the trees away from streets and buildings are scattered to match the land-cover canopy percentage. A measured tree keeps its place and its height, and it is evergreen or broadleaf as the 1 m land cover under it is (a guess where that shows no woods, and the land cover is from 2016). Its crown is one of two stock shapes, as tall as the tree and as wide as the canopy round its top suggests (read from the survey's grid: how much of it within 5 m stands at least half the tree's height), never reaching more than 0.6 of the way to the next tree. The woods are kept out of the strip cleared under a transmission line (30 m either side of one of 300 kV or more, 17 m of 100 to 299 kV, 12 m of 69 kV), as the utilities keep it; the trees the LiDAR measured there stay. Sidewalks and kerbs are drawn on both sides of every street that MassDOT's inventory gives a sidewalk on either side, where the land is at least a little built up; their width and the grass strip behind the kerb are invented. On cropland in the 1 m land cover, the colour (green with its crop, tilled brown or ripe gold) is drawn by chance in patches about 200 m across, which are not the real fields. A street MassDOT's inventory does not cover takes its width from its class and lane tags. Utility poles, their wires and the street lamps that some of them carry, parked cars, front walks, driveways and the cars on them are placed by rule along streets and beside houses, and none of them is surveyed. The signs, signals, hydrants, bus shelters, flagpoles, towers and poles that the map does have are stock shapes. A sign or signal stands at the kerb on the right of the traffic it is for, set back past the cross street at a junction, and a signal's arm reaches over the lanes coming its way. When the lights change is invented: green for 44% of a 70-second cycle, yellow for 6% and red for the rest, with the cross street half a cycle on. A marked crosswalk whose pattern the map does not give is drawn as a ladder, the pattern most often tagged here, and every crosswalk is about 3 m wide. A tower's shape follows its line's voltage and tags (a steel lattice at 115 kV and up, a wooden H-frame at 69 kV, a pole below that), and the wires' sag is drawn by rule. A field's or court's surface is the one the map gives where it gives one (2,131 of them); otherwise a tennis or pickleball court is a green hard court, a basketball court asphalt, a running track red and a field grass. The lines on courts and fields are each sport's standard markings fitted to the mapped outline (soccer's on lacrosse, field hockey and rugby fields too, several courts side by side where one outline holds them, and a half court where a basketball court is too short for a full one), and a football field's hash marks are high school ones. A mapped parking space gets its two side lines; lots whose spaces are not mapped are plain paving. A mast's or tower's shape is drawn by its kind and height: a mast 120 m tall or more is a guyed lattice mast in orange and white bands, a shorter one or a communication tower a three-legged lattice tower or, under 40 m (35 m for a tower), a monopole with panel antennas; chimneys, water towers and turbines are stock shapes scaled to their height. A turbine's rotor is two thirds of its height across where its diameter is not tagged, and every rotor faces the south-west, into the prevailing summer wind, its tips turning at 60 m/s. At night the 251 that are 61 m or taller carry red lights, flashing on top and, on those over 107 m, down the side, as the FAA asks of structures that tall; which ones are really lit, and how, is not known here. At night windows light up at random, and where they are too small to draw one by one they show as sparks of light on the walls. Past the distance where a building stops being drawn it stays as one point of light where it stands, as bright as its lit windows together, warm for a home and whiter for a shop or an office, and farther off still the ground glows with the same lights added up over every 92 m. The traffic is invented too: cars follow the mapped streets at about the speed limit, keep to the right, and pick a road at random where theirs ends. Buildings and other cars are solid. The boats are invented as well: sailing boats, launches and fishing boats wander wherever there is water enough, and the ferries keep to their mapped courses (see Ferries above) but not to their timetables.

A street's height is smoothed along its length so that a road keeps a road's grades, and a bump in the survey that is really a bridge passing overhead is ignored. A bridge's deck is at its measured height and everything else about it is invented: the parapets, the slab, the piers (one every 30 to 120 m, farther apart under a high deck) and the lamps. Where the National Bridge Inventory says a bridge is a truss, an arch, a suspension or cable-stayed bridge or a lift, bascule or swing span, that structure is drawn by rule from its design and the length of its main span, so the Sagamore and the Bourne have their arches and the Zakim its towers and stays, but the members are not measured. Where one deck runs under another for a long way, as the Tobin's lower deck does, it is set beneath the upper one by the gap measured where it comes out from under it. About 390 more spans that the map does not tag were found where the surveyed ground drops away under a road, and their decks are smooth curves from rim to rim because nothing was read for them. Where one of the older western surveys took a viaduct's deck for the ground (I-91 through Springfield, I-391 in Chicopee), the streets passing under it were read at the deck's height. Here they are drawn again between the heights around them, through a cut in that false ground. A deck steeper than 15% where it meets the road at its end was read on something else, and comes down to that road at 7% instead. Five ramps and a stretch of the Pike that the map makes bridges lie on the ground in the 2021 survey, at its interchanges with I-95 in Weston and I-495 in Hopkinton, so here they are roads on the ground, as any road newer than its survey is. A road tunnel runs underground, but only its route is real: its floor goes down from each portal at up to 5.5% to about 10 m under the ground over it (24 m under the harbour), the roads leading to a portal come down to it in a cut, and the tube, its lamps and walkways and the headwalls round its mouths are invented. Railway tracks stop where the real line goes underground. A river narrower than about 50 m that the land cover sees no water along (under trees, mostly) is drawn as a channel 8 m wide unless its width is tagged, which is rarely its true width, and a stream as one 1.6 m wide (2.2 m if it has a name, 4 m for a canal). A river takes one width for each stretch of it in a region, the middle of the widths measured along that stretch. Each railway track is drawn as one track on ballast. Runway and taxiway paint and lamps are generic. In the distance, where the ground is the 46 m grid, detail finer than the grid has about a metre and a half of invented texture. The ground eases down to the water along every shore over a band at least 30 m wide, so a bluff or a steep river bank is gentler here than the real one. Past the state line the neighbours' land runs on for about 460 m and falls to a border sea about two kilometres wide, then a quay, then the glass. The Atlantic and the bays and sounds off the coast are sea, as they are. The window's frame (girders every 6 km, beams every 750 m, glazing bars every 50 m) is invented, and so are the lamps along the quay and the lifts and marker lamps on the end caps. The stars are scattered at random and are not the real sky, the Milky Way (its bulge, its dust lanes, its star clouds), Andromeda and the Magellanic Clouds are painted by formula and are not where the real ones lie, and the faces of the Earth and Moon are invented. So is the twin: O'Neill's cylinders were meant to come in pairs turning opposite ways, and a second one rides 135 km off, so its windows and the lights of its towns wheel past ours once a rotation. You can drive on the glass.

On the radio, what a signal does over a few metres (the quick fades as you drive, a metre or two apart), the few decibels the terrain model misses, the electrical noise of a built-up town on AM, what a bridge overhead or a tunnel takes away (FM goes a little way down a tunnel, AM dies at the portal), the hiss, the static and the way one station breaks through another are drawn by rule. Skywave follows the FCC's median curve with slow invented fades, not a real night's sky. The Big Dig's tunnels carry twenty FM and ten AM stations on cable, and which ones is a guess: the twenty and ten strongest across Boston.
